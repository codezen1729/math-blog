\section*{Shishikura's Surgery Principles}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
Surgery is needed because we want to change the parameters of dynamics while keeping its topological core intact. Suppose a surgery deforms \(f\), by quasiconformal conjugacy, to \(\widetilde f\), and \(\widetilde f\) turns out to be quasiregular. The effort then goes into finding a \(\widetilde f\)-invariant Beltrami form, because of the following key lemma.
\setcounter{theorem}{47}\begin{lemma}[Key lemma for surgery]
If a quasiregular map \(\widetilde f\) satisfies \(\widetilde f^*\mu=\mu\) for a Beltrami form \(\mu\), then \(\widetilde f\) is quasiconformally conjugate to a holomorphic function. That is, there is a \(K\)-quasiconformal map \(\varphi\) such that \(\varphi\circ\widetilde f\circ\varphi^{-1}\) is holomorphic.
\end{lemma}
There are essentially two forms of surgery: \emph{soft surgery} and \emph{cut-and-paste surgery}. The former is mostly used to obtain a parametrized family of deformed rational maps, to construct something very specific and unrelated to the dynamics of the original map. Soft surgery is more like a postmortem, performed purely for purposes demanded elsewhere. Two instances are:
\begin{itemize}
\item The multiplier map \(\Lambda:\Omega\to\mathbb D\) of a hyperbolic component of \(\mathcal M\) is a conformal isomorphism.
% Source page 2
\item A rational map \(f:\widehat{\mathbb C}\to\widehat{\mathbb C}\) of degree at least two has no wandering domain.
\end{itemize}
In both cases, the dynamics of some \(g\) was deformed to obtain a family of maps, used strategically to establish the statements.
Cut-and-paste surgery, by contrast, is about the dynamics of \(f\), usually studying it by modelling it through deformations.
Either way, we must reach a stage where the surgery lemma applies. The following fundamental principles guide us by showing when surgery can be conducted.

\Needspace{15\baselineskip}
\setcounter{theorem}{48}\begin{theorem}[Principle 1: domain-wise surgery]\label{thm:cd12-domain-principle}
Let \(f:\widehat{\mathbb C}\to\widehat{\mathbb C}\) be quasiregular, and let \(p\ge1\). Suppose:
\begin{itemize}
\item \(U=\bigcup_{i=1}^pU_i\), where the \(U_i\) are pairwise disjoint open subsets of \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\), \(f(U_j)\subseteq U_{j+1}\) for \(1\le j<p\), and \(f(U_p)\subseteq U_1\).
\item \(\psi:U\to\widetilde U\) is quasiconformal.
\item \(H:\widetilde U\to\widetilde U\) is quasiregular, \(H^p\) is holomorphic, \(f|_U=\psi^{-1}\circ H\circ\psi\), and
\(\partial_{\bar z}f=0\) almost everywhere on \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus f^{-N}(U)\) for some \(N\ge0\).
\end{itemize}
% Source page 3
Then \(f\) is quasiconformally conjugate to a rational map: it is \emph{quasirational}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write \(\widetilde U_j=\psi(U_j)\); these sets, \(1\le j\le p\), are mutually disjoint. First define a Beltrami form \(\widetilde\mu\) on \(\widetilde U\) using \(H\): on \(\widetilde U_{p-k}\), for \(0\le k\le p-1\), set
\(\widetilde\mu=(H^{\circ k})^*\mu_0\). Since \(H^p\) is holomorphic, if \(H\) is \(K_H\)-quasiregular then
\(K(\widetilde\mu)\le K_H^{p-1}\).
Put \(\mu=\psi^*\widetilde\mu\) and spread it across the sphere. On \(f^{-n}(U)\), use \((f^{\circ n})^*\mu\), and on
\(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\bigcup_{n\ge0}f^{-n}(U)\), use \(\mu_0\). Finally,
\[
\|\mu\|_\infty\le k=\frac{K-1}{K+1},
\qquad K=K_\psi K_H^{p-1}K_f^N,
\]
where \(K_\psi,K_f\) are defined analogously to \(K_H\).
This is well-defined since
\[
U\subseteq f^{-1}(U)\subseteq f^{-2}(U)\subseteq\cdots
\subseteq\bigcup_{n\ge0}f^{-n}(U).
\]
The union is both forward and backward invariant, so \(\mu\) is \(f\)-invariant. The surgery lemma makes \(f\) quasirational.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
If \(f\) in Principle 1 is a deformation of \(\widetilde f\), then \(U_j\) corresponds to a Fatou component of \(\widetilde f\). It has no choice: if
\(J(\widetilde f)\cap\widetilde U_j\ne\varnothing\), then
\(\widetilde f(\widetilde U_j),\widetilde f^2(\widetilde U_j),\ldots,\widetilde f^n(\widetilde U_j)\) expand to \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\) almost everywhere.
\end{remark}

\setcounter{theorem}{49}\begin{theorem}[Principle 2: pointwise surgery]
Let \(f:\widehat{\mathbb C}\to\widehat{\mathbb C}\) be quasiregular and \(X\subset\widehat{\mathbb C}\) open. Suppose
\(\partial_{\bar z}f=0\) almost everywhere on \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus X\), \(f\) is \(K\)-quasiregular on \(X\), and there is \(N\ge1\) such that
\[
\max_{z\in X}\#\{n\geq0:f^{\circ n}(z)\in X\}\le N.
\]
Then \(f\) is quasirational.
\end{theorem}
% Source page 5
\begin{proof}
Denote the grand orbit of \(z\) by \(\operatorname{GO}(z)\). Then
\[
\widehat{\mathbb C}
=\bigsqcup_{z\in\widehat{\mathbb C}/\sim}\operatorname{GO}(z),
\qquad z\sim z'\ \Longleftrightarrow\ z'\in\operatorname{GO}(z).
\]
Define an invariant Beltrami form on each grand orbit. If
\(\operatorname{GO}(z)\cap X=\varnothing\), put \(\mu=\mu_0\) there. This is measurable on the grand orbit; moreover,
\(\operatorname{GO}(z)=\bigcup_{p,q\ge0}f^{-p}(f^q(z))\) is measurable.

If \(\operatorname{GO}(z)\cap X\ne\varnothing\), choose
\(x\in\operatorname{GO}(z)\cap X\). By hypothesis, there is \(m_x<\infty\) with \(f^{m_x}(x)=z_0\) and \(f^n(z_0)\notin X\) for every \(n\ge0\). Define the ellipse field on this grand orbit by
\[
\mu(w)=
\begin{cases}
\mu_0,&w=f^n(z_0),\ n\ge0,\\
(f^p)^*(\mu_0),&f^p(w)=f^q(z_0),\ q\ge0,\ p\ge1.
\end{cases}
\]
The same prescription can be written on the measurable sets determined by the finite entry and exit times from \(X\); these sets form a countable partition. Hence \(\mu\) is measurable on \(\widehat{\mathbb C}\).
Its modulus is controlled by the following chain of pullbacks:
\[
\mu(w)=(f^{p_1})^*\circ f^*\circ(f^{p_2})^*\circ f^*
\circ\cdots\circ(f^{p_{N'}})^*\circ f^*(\mu_0).
\]
The breaks are made at visits to \(X\) along a path from \(w\) to \(f^q(z_0)\). There are at most \(N\) visits, so the dilatation is bounded by \(K^N\), equivalently \(\|\mu\|_\infty<1\). By definition \(\mu\) is \(f\)-invariant; the surgery lemma makes \(f\) quasirational.
\end{proof}

% Source page 6
\begin{remark}
Sullivan generalized the first two principles. The resulting way to straighten the ellipse field of a quasiregular map is the following.
\end{remark}
\setcounter{theorem}{50}\begin{theorem}[Sullivan's straightening theorem]
Let \(f:\widehat{\mathbb C}\to\widehat{\mathbb C}\) be quasiregular. If there is \(K\) such that \(f^n\) is \(K\)-quasiregular for every \(n\ge1\), then \(f\) is quasirational.
\end{theorem}
