\section*{Orientation and Covering Maps}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN

% Source page 1
Here is an intuitive reason for requiring a manifold to be orientable.
Suppose you want to measure the volume of a M\"obius band or a cylinder,
that is, the set $[0,1]\times[0,1]$ with $(0,t)\sim(1,1-t)$, or with
$(0,t)\sim(1,t)$, respectively. In the absence of an ambient manifold
such as $\mathbb R^3$, these surfaces, as abstract objects, do not have faces.
Thus the volume of a page, as a manifold with boundary in its own right,
does not have to account for the two sides. It is essentially the area
of a page as a whole.
To measure the volume of a cylinder, draw a loop around it and measure
how much area you sweep as you traverse it. To keep track of the area
you have already swept, flag it.
\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering\includegraphics[width=0.52\textwidth]{m4-fig-01.pdf}
\end{figure}
But if your manifold is such that, even when you return to where you
started, you do not see your flag, as would happen for the M\"obius band,
you would end up overcounting. More precisely, the amount you overcount
will depend on the charts you choose. Therefore, having a consistent
sense of direction is crucial to being able to integrate on a manifold.
% Source page 2
We shall see some covering-space-theoretic implications of orientability.
We have seen covering space theory in calculating fundamental groups,
lifting holomorphic branches, studying covering spaces, classifying
manifolds, and pushing certain invariant metrics. We shall now see yet
another application: the Orientation Covering Theorem.

Given a covering map $\pi\colon E\to M$, there are fundamentally three
objects associated to it: $\pi_1(M)$, the fundamental group;
$\Aut_\pi(E)$, the group of deck transformations; and $E$, the covering space.
Let us see what these objects have to say about the orientability of $M$.
\setcounter{theorem}{13}\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:m4-deck-orientation}
Let $\pi\colon E\to M$ be a smooth normal covering map, where $E$ is a
connected, oriented smooth manifold and $M$ is a smooth manifold.
Then $M$ is orientable if and only if the action of $\Aut_\pi(E)$ on $E$
is orientation-preserving.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $E$ is connected, $M$ is connected. Let $O_E$ be the orientation on $E$.
Suppose first that $M$ is orientable.
Since $M$ is connected, there are exactly two orientations of $M$.
Fix $q\in E$. One of the orientations of $M$ is such that
$d\pi_q\colon T_qE\to T_{\pi(q)}M$ is orientation-preserving.
% Source page 3
Let $O_M$ be that orientation. We have $\pi^*O_M=O_E$ at $q$; since $E$
is connected, $\pi^*O_M=O_E$.
For $\varphi\in\Aut_\pi(E)$, we have $\pi\circ\varphi=\pi$, so
\[
 \varphi^*O_E=\varphi^*(\pi^*O_M)
 =(\pi\circ\varphi)^*O_M=\pi^*O_M=O_E.
\]
Thus $\varphi$ is orientation-preserving.

Conversely, suppose $\Aut_\pi(E)$ is orientation-preserving.
Fix $p\in M$, and let $U$ be a connected evenly covered neighbourhood of $p$.
Let $\sigma\colon U\to E$ be a smooth section, and induce the orientation
$\sigma^*O_E$. If we prove that this is a well-defined way of orienting
$M$, we are done. Let $\sigma_1\colon U\to E$ be another smooth section.
Since $\pi$ is normal, there is a $\varphi\in\Aut_\pi(E)$ such that
$\sigma_1(p)=\varphi(\sigma(p))$ and $\sigma_1=\varphi\circ\sigma$.
The latter follows from uniqueness of local sections of smooth covering
maps, that is, inverses of $\pi|_{\sigma(U)}$.
Since $\varphi$ is orientation-preserving,
$\sigma_1^*O_E=\sigma^*\varphi^*O_E=\sigma^*O_E$. Hence the orientation
is well-defined.
\end{proof}
From the action of $\Aut_\pi(E)$, one can
determine the orientability of $M$. But one can do better by considering
a particular covering space. Before we do that, here is an example.
% Source page 4
\setcounter{example}{5}\begin{example}
The map $q\colon S^n\to\mathbb{RP}^n$ is a covering map.
For $n>1$, $S^n$ is simply connected, and
$\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^n)=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\cong\Aut_q(S^n)$.
The nontrivial element of $\Aut_q(S^n)$ is $\alpha(x)=-x$, the antipodal map.
The map $\alpha$ is orientation-preserving if and only if $2\nmid n$.
Therefore $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable if and only if $2\nmid n$.
Also, $\mathbb{RP}^1$ is orientable.
\end{example}
\subsection{Orientation Covering}
We are now going to construct a particular covering map for $M$ to help
us analyse its orientability. Define
\[
 \widehat M:=\{(p,O_p):p\in M,\ O_p\text{ is an orientation of }T_pM\},
 \qquad
 \widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M,\quad\widehat\pi(p,O_p):=p.
\]
This is called the \emph{orientation covering} of $M$.
\setcounter{theorem}{14}\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:m4-smooth-cover}
Given the orientation covering $\widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M$, one can
give $\widehat M$ a smooth structure such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
 \setcounter{enumi}{0}\item $\widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M$ is a smooth covering map.
 % Source page 5
 \setcounter{enumi}{1}\item A connected open set $U\subset M$ is evenly covered by
 $\widehat\pi$ if and only if $U$ is orientable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $U\subset M$ be an orientable open set with orientation $O$. Set
\[
 \widehat U_O:=
 \{(p,O_p)\in\widehat M:p\in U,\ O_p\text{ orients }T_pM
 \text{ and is determined by }O\},
 \qquad O|_p=O_p.
\]
We show that $\{\widehat U_O\}_{(U,O)}$ forms a basis for $\widehat M$.
Let $(p,O_p)\in\widehat M$. Every chart at $p$ carries a natural orientation induced from its domain in $\mathbb R^n$. Thus there is
a pair $(U,O)$ with $O|_p=O_p$. Consequently $(p,O_p)\in\widehat U_O$, so
$\bigcup_{(U,O)}\widehat U_O=\widehat M$.

Let $(p,O_p)\in\widehat U_O\cap\widehat U'_{O'}$.
Then $O|_p=O'|_p=O_p$. Let $V$ be the component of $U\cap U'$ containing
$p$. The orientations $O|_V$ and $O'|_V$ agree at $p$, so $O|_V=O'|_V$.
Hence $\widehat V_{O|_V}\subset\widehat U_O\cap\widehat U'_{O'}$.
The restriction $\widehat\pi|_{\widehat U_O}$ is bijective onto $U$,
and the sets $\widehat U_O$ form a basis for $\widehat M$, while their images $U$ form a basis for $M$ because coordinate domains are orientable. Therefore $\widehat\pi$ is a local homeomorphism.

Let $(U,O)$ be such that $U$ is connected. Then $U$ can have only two
orientations, and
$\widehat\pi^{-1}(U)=\widehat U_O\sqcup\widehat U_{-O}$.
Both restrictions
$\widehat\pi|_{\widehat U_O}\colon\widehat U_O\cong U$ and
$\widehat\pi|_{\widehat U_{-O}}\colon\widehat U_{-O}\cong U$
are homeomorphisms. Thus $\widehat\pi$ is a covering map, and an orientable
connected open set is evenly covered.
There is a unique smooth structure on $\widehat M$ for which
$\widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M$ is a smooth covering map.
The space $\widehat M$ has the canonical orientation for which
$d\widehat\pi_{(p,O_p)}$ carries the orientation at $(p,O_p)$ to $O_p$.
To prove (b), it remains to show that if $U\subset M$ is an evenly covered
domain, then $U$ is orientable. Indeed, let
$\sigma\colon U\to\widehat M$ be an injective smooth local section.
Then $U$ can be endowed with the pullback orientation.
\end{proof}
We now state our main theorem, the reason for studying these orientation
covers.
% Source page 7
\setcounter{theorem}{15}\begin{theorem}[Orientation Covering Theorem]
Let $M$ be a connected smooth manifold and
$\widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M$ its orientation covering.
If $M$ is orientable, then $\widehat M$ has two components.
If $M$ is not orientable, then $\widehat M$ is connected.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Since $\widehat\pi$ is a covering map, its restriction
$\widehat\pi|_W$ to a component $W$ of $\widehat M$ is surjective.
The fibres of $\widehat\pi$ have cardinality $2$, so $\widehat M$ is either
connected or has two components.
If $M$ is orientable, then, being connected and open, it is evenly covered
by Proposition~\ref{prop:m4-smooth-cover}. Since
$|\widehat\pi^{-1}(\{p\})|=2$, we have
\[
 \widehat\pi^{-1}(M)=M^+\sqcup M^-=\widehat M,\qquad
 \widehat\pi|_{M^\pm}\colon M^\pm\cong M.
\]
If $M$ is not orientable, let $W$ be a component of $\widehat M$.
The restriction $\widehat\pi|_W$ is a covering map. Since $W$ is connected,
the degree of $\widehat\pi|_W$ is invariant on $M$, so its fibres have
cardinality either $1$ or $2$.
If their cardinality were $1$, then $\widehat\pi|_W\colon W\to M$ would
be a diffeomorphism, making $M$ orientable.
Therefore their cardinality is $2$, and surjectivity of
$\widehat\pi|_W$ implies $W=\widehat M$, which is connected.
\end{proof}
In hindsight, $\widehat M$ may seem tailor-made to capture orientability
by merely restating it in a different form on a made-up space $\widehat M$.
But that is not the case. The right way to think about it intuitively is
via an ambient manifold. Consider surfaces in $\mathbb R^3$, say a page.
The space $\widehat M$ is basically what you get after slicing the surface
along its extra dimension, which in $\mathbb R^3$ would be its thickness.
\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{m4-fig-02.pdf}
\end{figure}
You will not obtain something similar if you slice a M\"obius band.
There, after slicing, the orientation cover $\widehat M$ remains connected.
Beyond offering this geometric insight into orientability, there are
mathematical reasons substantiating the Orientation Covering Theorem.
% Source page 9
\setcounter{theorem}{16}\begin{proposition}
Let $M$ be a connected smooth manifold. If $\pi_1(M)$ has no subgroup of
index $2$, then $M$ is orientable. In particular, all simply connected
smooth manifolds are orientable.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}
If $M$ were simply connected and covered something orientable, say
$\pi\colon M\to M/{\sim}$ with $M/{\sim}$ orientable, then $\pi$, being
a smooth covering map, is a local diffeomorphism. Hence $M$ is orientable.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
Suppose $M$ is not orientable. Then $\widehat M$ is connected.
Let $\widehat\pi\colon\widehat M\to M$ be its orientation cover.
Choose $q\in\widehat M$, and let $p=\widehat\pi(q)\in M$.
Let $\alpha\colon\widehat M\to\widehat M$ interchange points in the same
fibre: if $\widehat\pi(q)=\widehat\pi(r)=p$, then $\alpha(q)=r$ and
$\alpha(r)=q$.
\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering\includegraphics[width=0.64\textwidth]{m4-fig-03.pdf}
\end{figure}
Note that $\alpha$ is smooth. Let $U$ be evenly covered, with
$\widehat\pi^{-1}(U)=U_0\sqcup U_1$. Then
\[
 \alpha|_{U_0}=(\widehat\pi|_{U_1})^{-1}\circ(\widehat\pi|_{U_0}),
 \qquad
 \alpha|_{U_1}=(\widehat\pi|_{U_0})^{-1}\circ(\widehat\pi|_{U_1}).
\]
Such $U_i$ cover $\widehat M$, so $\alpha$ is smooth on $\widehat M$, and
$\widehat\pi\circ\alpha=\widehat\pi$. By definition,
$\alpha\in\Aut_{\widehat\pi}(\widehat M)$.
If $\beta\in\Aut_{\widehat\pi}(\widehat M)$, then $\beta(q)$ is either
$q$ or $\alpha(q)$. Since deck transformations that agree at one point
agree everywhere, $\beta=\id_{\widehat M}$ or $\beta=\alpha$.
% Source page 10
But $\widehat M$ is connected, so
$\Aut_{\widehat\pi}(\widehat M)=\{\id_{\widehat M},\alpha\}$.
Since the action of $\Aut_{\widehat\pi}(\widehat M)$ is transitive,
$\widehat\pi$ is a normal covering, and
\[
 \pi_1(M)/\widehat\pi_*(\pi_1(\widehat M))
 \cong\Aut_{\widehat\pi}(\widehat M).
\]
Thus $\widehat\pi_*(\pi_1(\widehat M))$ is an index-$2$ subgroup of $\pi_1(M)$.
\end{proof}
