\section*{Attracting and Repelling Fixed Points}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
Let's look at the local behaviour of a holomorphic map $f\colon S\to S$, where $S$ is a Riemann surface. For this, it would ``suffice'' to study the local behaviour at fixed points of $f$. Recall that $S$ decomposes into components of basins with fractal-like boundaries (elements of the Julia set). Our goal is to describe the dynamics of $f$ locally in certain such basins. The underlying phenomenon governing these dynamics is the distribution of the critical points of $f$, especially for rational maps on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$. We shall explain this phenomenon.

Understanding the dynamics near fixed points amounts to finding a change of coordinates in which the orbits of $f$ are easy to describe, as expected. A general technique is to construct a local biholomorphism $\phi$ such that the following diagram commutes.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.32\textwidth]{cd2-fig-01.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.1}{fig:cd2-01}
\end{figure}
% Source page 2
That is, $\phi\circ f\circ\phi^{-1}=g$ locally around $0$, where $g$ is preferably a polynomial. One begins by finding maps $\{\phi_k\}_k$ such that $\phi_k\circ f=g\circ\phi_{k+1}$, then proves that $\phi_k\to\phi$ uniformly. Consequently,
\[
\lim_{k\to\infty}(\phi_k\circ f)=\lim_{k\to\infty}(g\circ\phi_{k+1}),
\quad\phi\circ f=g\circ\phi,
\quad\phi\circ f\circ\phi^{-1}=g.
\]
\setcounter{definition}{6}\begin{definition}
A fixed point is \emph{superattracting} if its multiplier $\lambda$ is $0$, geometrically attracting if $0<|\lambda|<1$, and repelling if $1<|\lambda|$.
\end{definition}
We deal with all fixed points except those for which $\lambda\in S^1$. First let us understand the dynamics locally, and then describe the phenomenon governing it.
\subsection{Geometrically attracting and repelling points}
For $f\in\Hol(S,S)$, let $\varphi\circ f\circ\varphi^{-1}=\widehat f$ be its coordinate representation locally around, and centred at, a fixed point. Here $\widehat f'(0)\notin\{0,1\}$. On sufficiently small domains, $\widehat f$ behaves like $\widehat f'(0)z=\lambda z$. If $\lambda<1$, $f$ pulls; if $\lambda>1$, $f$ pushes. More precisely:
% Author check: the explanatory sentence writes lambda rather than its modulus, and the preceding exclusion writes {0,1}.
% Source page 3
\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\setcounter{theorem}{10}\begin{theorem}[Koenigs linearisation]\label{cd2:koenigs}
If the multiplier satisfies $|\lambda|\notin\{0,1\}$, there is a map $\phi$, unique up to multiplication by a nonzero constant, such that the following diagram commutes.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.32\textwidth]{cd2-fig-02.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.2}{fig:cd2-02}
\end{figure}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Case 1: $|\lambda|<1$.} Identify $f$ with its local coordinate expression $\widehat f$. Let $0$ be the fixed point. We have $f(z)=\lambda z+O(z^2)$ in $\mathbb D_{r_0}$ for some $r_0<1$, and
\[
|f(z)|\le|\lambda z|+C|z|^2\le(|\lambda|+C|z|)|z|\qquad(|z|<r_0).
\]
Since $|\lambda|<1$, choosing $|z|$ small enough keeps $|\lambda|+C|z|$ within $\mathbb D$. More precisely, choose $0<r\le r_0$ such that $|\lambda|+Cr<1$, and put $c=|\lambda|+Cr$. Then $|f(z)|\le c|z|$ for every $z\in\mathbb D_r$, so $f(z)\in\mathbb D_r$ and
$|f^{\circ n}(z)|\le c^n|z|<c^nr\to0$ as $n\to\infty$.
% Source page 4
This is precisely the push/pull we were discussing. There is $r<1$ such that $|f(z)|<c|z|$ on $\mathbb D_r$, with $c<1$. Let $\{z_n\}_n$ be the orbit of $z_0\in\mathbb D_r$. All these orbits converge to the origin, since $|z_n|\le rc^n$, and $|z_{n+1}-\lambda z_n|\le C|z_n|^2\le Cr^2c^{2n}$. Set $w_n=z_n/\lambda^n$. Then
\begin{align*}
|w_{n+1}-w_n|
&=\left|\frac{z_{n+1}}{\lambda^{n+1}}-\frac{z_n}{\lambda^n}\right|
=\frac{|z_{n+1}-\lambda z_n|}{|\lambda|^{n+1}}\\
&\le\frac{Cr^2c^{2n}}{|\lambda|^{n+1}}
=\frac{Cr^2}{|\lambda|}\left(\frac{c^2}{|\lambda|}\right)^n.
\end{align*}
Choose $c$ with $c^2<|\lambda|<c$. The sequence $\{w_n\}_n$ is Cauchy, and its geometric bound is independent of $z_0$. The map $\phi_n(z_0)=f^{\circ n}(z_0)/\lambda^n$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb D_r$, with $\phi_n(f(z_0))=\lambda\phi_{n+1}(z_0)$. Taking $\phi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi_n$ gives $\phi\circ f=\lambda\phi$. Also, $\phi_n'(0)=1$, so $\phi'(0)=1$, and $\phi$ is a local conformal isomorphism around $0$.

\emph{Case 2: $|\lambda|>1$.} Since $f'(0)\ne0$, $f$ is locally invertible. Its local inverse has multiplier $0<|1/\lambda|<1$ at $0$.
% Source page 5
For uniqueness, if $\phi,\psi$ are two such Koenigs maps, then $\psi\circ\phi^{-1}$ commutes with $z\mapsto\lambda z$. If $\psi\circ\phi^{-1}(w)=\sum_{n\ge1}b_nw^n$, then $\lambda b_n=b_n\lambda^n$. Thus $b_k=0$ for every $k\ge2$, and $\psi=b_1\phi$.
\end{proof}
\subsection{Superattracting fixed points}
If $f'(0)=0$, then locally $f(z)=a_nz^n+a_{n+1}z^{n+1}+\cdots$. Thus $f$ has a zero of order $n$ at $0$, and in a suitable domain $f(z)=h(z)^nz^n$. Close to the origin in this domain, $f$ acts almost like the polynomial $a_nz^n$. The following theorem makes a much sharper claim.
\setcounter{theorem}{11}\begin{theorem}[B\"ottcher]\label{cd2:bottcher}
If $0$ is a superattracting fixed point of $f$, with a zero of order $n$ at $0$, there is a map $\phi$, unique up to multiplication by an $(n-1)$st root of unity, such that the following diagram commutes.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.32\textwidth]{cd2-fig-03.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.3}{fig:cd2-03}
\end{figure}
\end{theorem}
% Source page 6
\begin{proof}
Locally around $0$, write $f(z)=b_0z^n+b_1z^{n+1}+b_2z^{n+2}+\cdots$. Since $cf(z/c)=b_0z^n/c^{n-1}+\cdots$, choosing $c^{n-1}=b_0$ allows us to assume that the leading coefficient is $1$.

Choose $r$ small enough that $f$ maps $\mathbb D_r$ into itself. The orbit of $z$ has the form
\[
f^{\circ k}(z)=\left(z^n\left(1+\sum_{j\ge1}b_jz^j\right)\right)^{\circ k}
=z^{n^k}(1+n^{k-1}b_1z+\cdots).
\]
Set $\phi_k(z)=z(1+(b_1/n)z+\cdots)$, taking the local root so that
$(\phi_k(z))^{n^k}=z^{n^k}(1+n^{k-1}b_1z+\cdots)=f^{\circ k}(z)$.
Direct substitution gives $\phi_k(f(z))=(\phi_{k+1}(z))^n$.

It remains to prove that $\phi_k$ converges uniformly. Conjugate $f$ and $\phi_k$ by $(z\mapsto e^z)|_{\mathbb H_r}$. Let $\eta(z)=\sum_{j\ge1}b_jz^j$. Then
\[
F(z)=\log f(e^z)=\log\bigl(e^{nz}(1+\eta(z))\bigr)
=nz+\log(1+\eta(z)),
\]
so $|F(z)-nz|=|\log(1+\eta(z))|<\log2<1$ whenever $|\eta(z)|<1/2$.
% Author check: source uses eta(z), although the preceding definition would give eta(e^z) after the exponential substitution.
% Source page 7
Set $\Phi_k(z)=\log\phi_k(e^z)=F^{\circ k}(z)/n^k$. Then
\[
|\Phi_{k+1}(z)-\Phi_k(z)|
=\frac{|F^{\circ(k+1)}(z)-nF^{\circ k}(z)|}{n^{k+1}}
<\frac1{n^{k+1}}.
\]
This is again a uniform bound. Since $e^z\colon\mathbb H_r\to\mathbb D_r$ is distance-reducing,
\[
|\phi_{k+1}(z)-\phi_k(z)|
<|\log\phi_{k+1}(e^z)-\log\phi_k(e^z)|<\frac1{n^{k+1}}.
\]
Thus $\{\phi_k\}_k$ has a uniform limit $\phi$. Uniqueness again follows because $\phi\circ\psi^{-1}$ commutes with $z\mapsto z^n$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
A crucial fact is that $f(z)\ne0$ for $z\in\mathbb D_r\setminus\{0\}$, so $\log f(e^z)$ makes sense. This is a useful technique for bounding quantities: shift one's domain to another hyperbolic surface and demand the luxuries of Pick's theorem.
\end{remark}
% Source page 8
Now that we understand the local dynamics near geometrically attracting, repelling, and superattracting points, to what extent can we extend this knowledge? In other words, on how large a domain is the nature of $f$ near these fixed points as we asserted? Let us globalise this local behaviour as far as nature allows, primarily for rational maps on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$.
\subsection{Global linearisation}
Koenigs' function $\phi$ extends holomorphically to the basin of attraction $\mathcal A$ of the fixed point $\widehat p$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.32\textwidth]{cd2-fig-04.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.4}{fig:cd2-04}
\end{figure}
\begin{proof}
The formula $\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{\circ n}(z)/\lambda^n$ is valid on $\mathcal A$, since it converges uniformly on compact sets. For every $z\in\mathcal A$, there is a neighbourhood $\overline U\subseteq\mathcal A$ on which $f^{\circ n}$ converges uniformly to $\widehat p$. Thus there is $N$ such that $f^{\circ n}(z)\in U$, the domain of $\phi$, for every $n\ge N$ and $z\in\overline U$. Then
% Source page 9
\[
\frac{\phi(f^{\circ N}(z))}{\lambda^N}
=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f^{\circ n}(z)}{\lambda^n}.
\]
Indeed, $\phi$ extends to $\mathcal A$.
\end{proof}
The local inverse $\psi=\phi^{-1}$ of Koenigs' function extends to an entire function when $\widehat p$ is a repelling fixed point.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.36\textwidth]{cd2-fig-05.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.5}{fig:cd2-05}
\end{figure}
The proof follows the same lines: drag values in $\mathbb C$ into $\mathbb D_\varepsilon$ for small $\varepsilon$, invert via $\phi^{-1}$, and compose with a suitable iterate of $f$ to make the diagram commute. Locally,
\[
\psi(w)=f^{\circ N}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac w{\lambda^N}\right)\right),\qquad
f\circ\psi(w)=f^{\circ(N+1)}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac w{\lambda^N}\right)\right),
\]
while $\psi\circ\lambda(w)=f^{\circ N}\bigl(\phi^{-1}(w/\lambda^{N-1})\bigr)$.
\begin{remark}
Such an extension is not possible for B\"ottcher coordinates: to make the diagram commute one must deal with $p\mapsto\sqrt[n^k]{\phi(f^{\circ k}(p))}$, but the root is not well-defined. One can, however, extend $|\phi|$, since roots in $\mathbb R_+$ are well-defined.
\end{remark}
% Source page 10
\subsection{The phenomenon of critical points: analytic aspects}
Critical points govern rational dynamics in three significant ways:
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{0}\item Critical points form the boundary beyond which Koenigs and B\"ottcher coordinates cannot be extended biholomorphically (analytic).
\setcounter{enumi}{1}\item Critical points determine the number of attracting periodic points or orbits (analytic).
\setcounter{enumi}{2}\item They determine the topological shape of the Julia set (topological; we shall see this in another post).
\end{enumerate}
The analysis of a rational map is essentially determined by its roots and poles, whereas in dynamics its critical points become essential. We address these phenomena one by one. Unless otherwise stated, $f$ is a rational map with $\deg(f)\ge2$, and the surface is $\widehat{\mathbb C}$.
\subsubsection{Biholomorphic extension}
\setcounter{definition}{7}\begin{definition}
Given an attracting fixed point $\widehat p$ of a map $f\colon S\to S$, let $\mathcal A_0$ denote the connected component of its basin of attraction that contains $\widehat p$.
\end{definition}
\setcounter{theorem}{12}\begin{theorem}\label{cd2:maximal}
There is a maximal $r$ such that $\psi\colon\mathbb D_r\to\mathcal A_0$ is a holomorphic inverse of the Koenigs coordinate $\phi$. The closed disc $\overline{\mathbb D_r}$ and $\psi(\overline{\mathbb D_r})$ are homeomorphic, and $\psi(\partial\mathbb D_r)$ contains a critical point of $f$.
\end{theorem}
% Source page 11
\begin{proof}
Suppose $\phi^{-1}$ extends biholomorphically to an entire function. We would then have the following commutative diagram, implying that $f|_{\psi(\mathbb C)}$ is injective.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.38\textwidth]{cd2-fig-06.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.6}{fig:cd2-06}
\end{figure}
Since $\deg(f)\ge2$, $J(f)\ne\varnothing$. By Picard's theorem, $|\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\psi(\mathbb C)|\le2$, because $\psi$ is entire. But $J(f)$ is either connected or has uncountably many components, so $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\psi(\mathbb C)$ is a single point. By invariance, $f$ is injective on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$, which is impossible when $\deg(f)\ge2$. There is consequently a maximal $r$ such that the following diagram commutes.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.42\textwidth]{cd2-fig-07.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.7}{fig:cd2-07}
\end{figure}
The set $\lambda\overline{\mathbb D_r}$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb D_r$, and the image of $\mathbb D_r$ under multiplication by $\lambda$ lies in $\lambda\overline{\mathbb D_r}$. Thus there is a compact $K\subset U$ with $f(U)\subset K$, and
$f(\overline U)\subseteq\overline{f(U)}\subseteq K\subset U\subset\mathcal A$.
% Source page 12
By invariance, $\overline U\subset\mathcal A$, so $\phi$ is holomorphic on $\overline U$, since it extends to the whole basin. Let $w_0\in\partial\mathbb D_r$ and suppose $\partial U$ contains no critical point of $f$. Then $f$ is locally invertible at each $z_0\in\partial U$. But $\psi(w_0)$ may not yet be well-defined: the curve $t\mapsto\psi(tw_0)$ as $t\to1$ might accumulate at several points.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.78\textwidth]{cd2-fig-08.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.8}{fig:cd2-08}
\end{figure}
Let $z_0\in\partial U$ be an accumulation point of $\psi(tw_0)$ as $t\to1$. Since $f$ has a local inverse there, define, on a neighbourhood $V$ of $w_0$,
\[
\psi(w)=f^{-1}(\psi(\lambda w))\qquad(w\in V).
\]
Do this at every point. The identity theorem patches the local pieces over the compact set $\partial\mathbb D_r$, extending $\psi$ biholomorphically to a larger disc. That the extension is biholomorphic is again a consequence of the identity theorem.
\end{proof}
Thus critical points form the obstruction that stops, or contains, biholomorphic extension within a domain in $\mathcal A$.
% Source page 13
\setcounter{theorem}{13}\begin{theorem}\label{cd2:maximal-bottcher}
There is a maximal $0<r\le1$ such that the B\"ottcher coordinate $\phi$ has a biholomorphic inverse on $\mathbb D_r$. If $r=1$, then $\mathbb D\cong\mathcal A_0$, and the superattracting point $\widehat p$ is the only critical point in $\mathcal A$. Otherwise there is another critical point in $\mathcal A_0$, on $\partial\psi(\mathbb D_r)$.
\end{theorem}
% Author check: source says the only critical point in the full basin A, rather than A_0.
\begin{proof}
As before, a maximal $r\le1$ exists. For the following diagram to commute, the image of $\mathbb D_r$ under $z\mapsto z^n$, and all its subsequent iterates, must lie in $\mathbb D_r$, since $f^{\circ n}(\mathcal A_0)\subseteq\mathcal A_0$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.38\textwidth]{cd2-fig-09.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD2.9}{fig:cd2-09}
\end{figure}
Before, we had the luxury of the extended map $\phi$ on $\mathcal A$, so continuation of the holomorphic inverse by the identity theorem was a biholomorphic extension. We do not have that now, and must make do with the extension of $|\phi|$.

The map $\psi$ has no critical points in $\mathbb D_r$, since $\psi'(w)=0$ implies
$\psi'(w^n)=(f\circ\psi)'(w)=f'(\psi(w))\psi'(w)=0$.
Thus $w^n$, and all $w^{n^k}$, would be critical points, which is impossible by the identity theorem. Hence $\psi$ is locally injective (biholomorphic).
% Author check: source chain-rule formula omits the factor n w^{n-1}; retained.

The set $\{(w_1,w_2)\in\mathbb D_r\times\mathbb D_r:\psi(w_1)=\psi(w_2)\}$ is closed. Thus choose $|w_1|=|w_2|$ minimal
% Source page 14
with $\psi(w_1)=\psi(w_2)$. Since $|\phi|$ extends to $\mathcal A$, we have $|\phi(\psi(w))|=|w|$ for all $w\in\mathbb D_\varepsilon$, the domain of biholomorphy. By the identity theorem, $|\phi(\psi(w))|=|w|$ for every $w\in\mathbb D_r$. Consequently $\psi(w_1)=\psi(w_2)$ implies $|w_1|=|w_2|$.

Let $w_1'\in B_\varepsilon(w_1)$. The set $\psi^{-1}(\psi(B_\varepsilon(w_1)))$ contains $w_2$, so $\psi(B_\varepsilon(w_1))\cap\psi(B_\varepsilon(w_2))\ne\varnothing$. There are therefore $w_1',w_2'$ with $|w_1'|<|w_1|$ and $\psi(w_1')=\psi(w_2')$, a contradiction. Thus $\psi$ is injective, hence biholomorphic onto $\psi(\mathbb D_r)$.

If $r=1$ and $\psi(\mathbb D)\subsetneq\mathcal A_0$, take $z_0\in\partial U\cap\mathcal A_0$. Approximate $z_0$ by $\psi(w_j)$. Then $|\phi(\psi(w_j))|=|w_j|\to1$, so $|\phi(z_0)|=1$, and $z_0$ does not tend to $0$, contradicting $z_0\in\mathcal A_0$.

If $r<1$, just as before, a critical point on the boundary prevents extension of $\psi$ beyond its maximal disc $\mathbb D_r$.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
We defer the homeomorphism part to the next post, where we explore topological properties, including continuous extension to $\partial\mathbb D_r$ of the inverses of B\"ottcher and Koenigs coordinates, and much more.
\end{remark}
\subsubsection{The number of attracting periodic points}
% Source page 15
\setcounter{theorem}{14}\begin{theorem}\label{cd2:finite-attractors}
There are finitely many attracting periodic orbits.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For fixed points this follows because all immediate basins contain critical points, and a rational map has finitely many critical points. Given an attracting orbit $z_0\mapsto z_1\mapsto\cdots\mapsto z_0$ in $n$ steps, consider $f^{\circ n}$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Alternate proof]
Let $\widehat p\in O$ be an attracting fixed point, where $O$ is its immediate basin. If $O$ has no critical value of $f$, then there is a neighbourhood $U$ of $\widehat p$ and, for every $k\ge1$, a map $g_k\colon U\to\widehat{\mathbb C}$ inverse to $f^{\circ k}$. The family $\{g_k\}_k$ is normal, but $g_k'(\widehat p)$ is unbounded, since it is $\lambda^k$ with $\lambda>1$.
\end{proof}
Critical points therefore keep count of the number of attracting periodic orbits. Let us try to extend this theorem to noncompact Riemann surfaces and nonrational maps.
\setcounter{definition}{8}\begin{definition}
For $f\colon S\to S'$, a \emph{critical value} is the image under $f$ of a critical point.
\end{definition}
\setcounter{definition}{9}\begin{definition}
If there is a curve $\gamma\colon[0,1)\to S$ that eventually leaves compact sets, but $\lim_{t\to1}f(\gamma(t))=v$, then $v$ is an \emph{asymptotic value}.
\end{definition}
% Source page 16
\setcounter{theorem}{15}\begin{theorem}
Every proper holomorphic self-map $f\colon S\to S$ has a critical value in every immediate basin of an attracting orbit.
\end{theorem}
