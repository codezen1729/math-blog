\section*{Small Gaps between Primes}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
% Source page 1
Let $\mathbb P$ denote the set of primes. Enumerate them in increasing
order as $p_1,p_2,p_3,\ldots,p_n,\ldots$, where $p_n$ denotes the $n$th prime.
We ask a very basic question about the additive distribution of $\mathbb P$.
Fix a width parameter $h$. How many primes can you find in the interval
$[x,x+h]$, and how often?

Let us make the question concrete. If $[x,x+h]$ contains $k+1$ primes and $p_n$ is the smallest, then $p_{n+k}-p_n\le h$.
It therefore suffices to study
\[H_m:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+m}-p_n).\]
When $H_m<\infty$, infinitely many intervals of bounded length contain at least $m+1$ primes.
% Author check: the source's off-by-one counts and bounded-set argument are retained.
Studying the quantities $\{H_m\}_m$ is difficult because of the additive nature of the problem.
Primes appear naturally only as factors, so they are suited for
multiplicative studies. Regardless, there are deep conjectures about this.
The best known of these is the
% Source page 2
Twin Prime Conjecture.
\setcounter{theorem}{17}\begin{conjecture}[Twin Prime Conjecture]
$H_1=2$, or equivalently, there are infinitely many twin primes.
\end{conjecture}
Modern sieve-theoretic tools give highly nontrivial finite bounds for $H_m$.
The first breakthrough came from Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m,
who developed a sieve technique to find at least two primes in an interval
$[a,b]$.
\paragraph{2005: Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m.}
They proved
\[
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0.
\]
In fact, they proved the stronger version
$p_{n+1}-p_n\le(\log p_n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}$ infinitely often.
\paragraph{2013: Yitang Zhang.}
$\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)=H_1\le70{,}000{,}000$.
\paragraph{2013: Polymath.}
$H_1\le4{,}680$. Tao organised a Polymath project, inviting mathematicians
from all over to optimise Zhang's technique.
It turns out that Zhang, and subsequently the Polymath group, used
Deligne's theorem, which proves the Riemann hypothesis for finite fields.
% Source page 3
In 2013, James Maynard found an independent route using elementary sieve methods.
\paragraph{2013: J. Maynard, independently.}
$H_1\le600$. Subsequently, Maynard and Tao's group joined forces.
\paragraph{2014: Maynard and Polymath.}
$H_1\le246$.
They were also able to prove the following bound for $H_m$.
\paragraph{2015: Maynard and Tao's group.}
$H_m\ll m^3e^{4m}$, asserting for the first time that $\{H_m\}_m$ is a
sequence of integers.
% Author check: the historical narrative and dated claims are reproduced, not updated.
We now focus on the 2005 work of Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m: first motivate $\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log p_n=0$, then examine the technique behind its proof.
Since the problem is additive and the multiplicative structure of primes does not appear directly, we model $\mathbb P$ by a sequence just as
sparse as $\mathbb P$. Collect the numbers $\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}$.
In this set there are $n/\log n$ primes.
% Source page 4
The probability of $n$ being prime is therefore $1/\log n$.
\begin{remark}[Caution]
This heuristic model must be interpreted cautiously.
Firstly, the probability of $n$ being prime in $\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}$ is
$1/\log(n+1)$, and in $\{1,2,\ldots,n+k\}$ it is $1/\log(n+k)$,
yet we choose $1/\log n$.
Secondly, if a number $p>2$ is prime, then $p+1$ is certainly not prime,
since $2\mid p+1$. Thus the probability that $p+1$ is prime is $0$,
but according to our model it has nonzero probability $1/\log(p+1)>0$.
\end{remark}
We model primes as follows:
$\widehat{\mathbb P}:=\{p:X(p)=1\}$, where $X(p)\in\{0,1\}$ and
$\mathbb P(X(p)=1)=1/\log p$.
This is Cram\'er's model of primes.
Let us use it to count primes in $(p+\alpha\log p,p+\beta\log p)$ for a
large prime $p$. For some $h\in(\alpha\log p,\beta\log p)$, the probability
\[
 \mathbb P\bigl(X(p+i)=0,\ 1\le i\le h-1,\ \text{and }X(p+h)=1\bigr)
\]
is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\left(1-\frac1{\log(p+1)}\right)
 \left(1-\frac1{\log(p+2)}\right)\cdots
 \left(1-\frac1{\log(p+h-1)}\right)\frac1{\log(p+h)}\\
 &\hspace{3em}\sim
 \left(1-\frac1{\log p}\right)^{h-1}\frac1{\log p},
 \end{aligned}
\]
if $h$ is small compared with $p$.
% Author check: X(p+h)=0 is written in the event, while the product uses the probability of X(p+h)=1.
By Taylor expansion, $e^{-1/\log p}\sim1-1/\log p$.
Combining these approximations, according to the random
% Source page 5
model, the probability that the next prime lies in
$(p+\alpha\log p,p+\beta\log p)$ is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\sum_{\alpha\log p<h<\beta\log p}
 \mathbb P\bigl(X(p+i)=0,\ 1\le i\le h-1,\ X(p+h)=1\bigr)\\
 &\qquad\sim
 \sum_{\alpha\log p<h<\beta\log p}
 e^{-(h-1)/\log p}\frac1{\log p}
 \approx\int_\alpha^\beta e^{-t}\,dt.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus the probability that $(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)/\log p$ lies between
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ is
$\int_\alpha^\beta e^{-t}\,dt=e^{-\alpha}-e^{-\beta}$.
Set $\alpha=0$ and take $\beta$ very small.
With positive probability, $(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)/\log p\in[0,\varepsilon)$
for any $\varepsilon>0$. This suggests the following.
\setcounter{theorem}{18}\begin{conjecture}
$\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$.
\end{conjecture}
Erd\H{o}s was the first to make nontrivial progress. He proved
$\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log p_n\le1$.
This was improved to $0.24$. Finally, in 2005, Goldston, Pintz, and
Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m proved the following.
% Source page 6
\setcounter{theorem}{19}\begin{theorem}[Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m]
\label{thm:m5-gpy}
$\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$.
\end{theorem}
The quantity $\log p$ is usually called the \emph{average gap} because,
according to the Prime Number Theorem, there is at least one prime on
average in each interval
$(k\log n,(k+1)\log n)$, where $0\le k\le n/\log n$.
With Theorem~\ref{thm:m5-gpy}, one can ask whether
$\liminf_{p\to\infty}(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)<\infty$, that is, whether
bounded gaps between primes exist.


\setcounter{theorem}{20}\begin{conjecture}
$\displaystyle\limsup_{p\to\infty}
\frac{p_{\mathrm{next}}-p}{(\log p)^2}=1$.
\end{conjecture}
Consider the sequence of consecutive numbers $m!+2,m!+3,\ldots,m!+m$.
If $m!\sim x$, then the length of $(m!+2,m!+m)$ is
$\sim\log x/\log\log x$, which is far less than $(\log x)^2$.
In fact, it is dominated even by the average gap.

% Source page 7
Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m aimed for the much stronger bound $\boxed{H_1\le16}$.
Their strategy begins with an admissible tuple.
Let $\mathcal H=\{h_1,h_2,\ldots,h_k\}$, where $h_1<h_2<\cdots<h_k$.
It is called an \emph{admissible $k$-tuple} if it misses at least one congruence class modulo every prime.
% Author check: minimal diameter is included in the source's definition of admissibility.
Given an admissible $k$-tuple, ask whether two primes can occur in $\{n+h_1,n+h_2,\ldots,n+h_k\}$, because avoiding a residue class modulo every prime removes the obvious congruence obstruction.
Suppose one can find a sequence $\{a(n)\}_n$ of nonnegative numbers such
that, for every $j=1,2,\ldots,k$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)
 >\frac1k\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n).
 \tag{$\star$}\label{eq:m5-star}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^k\left(\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)\right)
 =\sum_{x\le n\le2x}\#\{1\le j\le k:n+h_j\in\mathbb P\}\,a(n)
 >\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n).
\]
Hence $\#\{1\le j\le k:n+h_j\in\mathbb P\}\ge2$, giving
$p_{n+1}-p_n\le\diam(\{h_j\}_{j=1}^k)$.

% Source page 11
This attempt to prove $H_1\le16$ led to subsequent improvements and the following bounds.
\setcounter{theorem}{22}\begin{theorem}[Polymath]
$H_1\le246$.
\end{theorem}
\setcounter{theorem}{23}\begin{theorem}[Assuming the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture]
$H_1\le6$.
\end{theorem}
The \emph{parity obstruction} explains why this family of sieve methods alone cannot improve the conditional bound below $6$.
