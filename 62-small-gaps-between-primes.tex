\section*{Small Gaps between Primes}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
\begin{center}\small S. Viswanathan\end{center}
% Source page 1
Let $\mathbb P$ denote the set of primes. Enumerate them in increasing
order as $p_1,p_2,p_3,\ldots,p_n,\ldots$, where $p_n$ denotes the $n$th prime.
We ask a very basic question about the additive distribution of $\mathbb P$.
Fix a width parameter $h$. How many primes can you find in the interval
$[x,x+h]$, and how often?

Let us begin by making the question concrete. If there are $k$ primes in
$[x,x+h]$, of which $p_n$ is the smallest, then $p_{n+k}-p_n\le h$.
It would suffice to study $H_m:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+m}-p_n)$.
Set $\widehat H_m:=\{p_{n+m}-p_n:p_n\in\mathbb P\}$.
If $\widehat H_m$ is bounded, then, by the Bolzano--Weierstrass theorem,
so is $H_m$. Further, one can say that in infinitely many intervals of
size $H_m$ there are at least $m$ primes.
% Author check: the source's off-by-one counts and bounded-set argument are retained.
Studying $\{H_m\}_m$, assuming that its terms are indeed numbers, is
extremely hard because of the additive nature of the problem.
Primes appear naturally only as factors, so they are suited for
multiplicative studies. Regardless, there are deep conjectures about this.
Of them all, the
% Source page 2
famous, perhaps even mythical, one is the Twin Prime Conjecture.
\setcounter{theorem}{17}\begin{conjecture}[Twin Prime Conjecture]
$H_1=2$, or equivalently, there are infinitely many twin primes.
\end{conjecture}
Recently, by deploying advanced sieve-theoretic tools, highly nontrivial
bounds for $H_m$ have been found, establishing that they are indeed numbers!
The first breakthrough came from Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m,
who developed a sieve technique to find at least two primes in an interval
$[a,b]$.
\paragraph{2005: Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m.}
They proved
\[
 \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0.
\]
In fact, they proved the stronger version
$p_{n+1}-p_n\le(\log p_n)^{1/2+\varepsilon}$ infinitely often.
\paragraph{2013: Yitang Zhang.}
$\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)=H_1\le70{,}000{,}000$.
\paragraph{2013: Polymath.}
$H_1\le4{,}680$. Tao organised a Polymath project, inviting mathematicians
from all over to optimise Zhang's technique.
It turns out that Zhang, and subsequently the Polymath group, used
Deligne's theorem, which proves the Riemann hypothesis for finite fields.
% Source page 3
A postdoc named James Maynard found a shortcut and surprised everyone.
He used elementary sieve methods.
\paragraph{2013: J. Maynard, independently.}
$H_1\le600$. Subsequently, Maynard and Tao's group joined forces.
\paragraph{2014: Maynard and Polymath.}
$H_1\le246$.
They were also able to prove the following bound for $H_m$.
\paragraph{2015: Maynard and Tao's group.}
$H_m\ll m^3e^{4m}$, asserting for the first time that $\{H_m\}_m$ is a
sequence of integers.
% Author check: the historical narrative and dated claims are reproduced, not updated.
This talk is about the 2005 work of Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m.
Let us heuristically motivate
$\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log p_n=0$ and then see the technique
that went into its proof.
Since the problem is additive and the role of primes as factors does not
seem to be to the fore, we shall model $\mathbb P$ by a sequence just as
sparse as $\mathbb P$. Collect the numbers $\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}$.
In this set there are $n/\log n$ primes.
% Source page 4
The probability of $n$ being prime is therefore $1/\log n$.
\begin{remark}[Caution]
These arguments must be taken with a pinch of salt.
Firstly, the probability of $n$ being prime in $\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}$ is
$1/\log(n+1)$, and in $\{1,2,\ldots,n+k\}$ it is $1/\log(n+k)$,
yet we choose $1/\log n$.
Secondly, if a number $p>2$ is prime, then $p+1$ is certainly not prime,
since $2\mid p+1$. Thus the probability that $p+1$ is prime is $0$,
but according to our model it has nonzero probability $1/\log(p+1)>0$.
\end{remark}
We model primes as follows:
$\widehat{\mathbb P}:=\{p:X(p)=1\}$, where $X(p)\in\{0,1\}$ and
$\mathbb P(X(p)=1)=1/\log p$.
This is Cram\'er's model of primes.
Let us use it to count primes in $(p+\alpha\log p,p+\beta\log p)$ for a
large prime $p$. For some $h\in(\alpha\log p,\beta\log p)$, the probability
\[
 \mathbb P\bigl(X(p+i)=0,\ 1\le i\le h-1,\ \text{and }X(p+h)=0\bigr)
\]
is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\left(1-\frac1{\log(p+1)}\right)
 \left(1-\frac1{\log(p+2)}\right)\cdots
 \left(1-\frac1{\log(p+h-1)}\right)\frac1{\log(p+h)}\\
 &\hspace{3em}\sim
 \left(1-\frac1{\log p}\right)^{h-1}\frac1{\log p},
 \end{aligned}
\]
if $h$ is small compared with $p$.
% Author check: X(p+h)=0 is written in the event, while the product uses the probability of X(p+h)=1.
By Taylor expansion, $e^{-1/\log p}\sim1-1/\log p$.
Combining these approximations, according to the random
% Source page 5
model, the probability that the next prime lies in
$(p+\alpha\log p,p+\beta\log p)$ is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 &\sum_{\alpha\log p<h<\beta\log p}
 \mathbb P\bigl(X(p+i)=0,\ 1\le i\le h-1,\ X(p+h)=0\bigr)\\
 &\qquad\sim
 \sum_{\alpha\log p<h<\beta\log p}
 e^{-(h-1)/\log p}\frac1{\log p}
 \approx\int_\alpha^\beta e^{-t}\,dt.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Thus the probability that $(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)/\log p$ lies between
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ is
$\int_\alpha^\beta e^{-t}\,dt=e^{-\alpha}-e^{-\beta}$.
Set $\alpha=0$ and take $\beta$ very small.
With positive probability, $(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)/\log p\in[0,\varepsilon)$
for any $\varepsilon>0$. This suggests the following.
\setcounter{theorem}{18}\begin{conjecture}
$\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$.
\end{conjecture}
Erd\H{o}s was the first to make nontrivial progress. He proved
$\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)/\log p_n\le1$.
This was improved to $0.24$. Finally, in 2005, Goldston, Pintz, and
Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m proved the following.
% Source page 6
\setcounter{theorem}{19}\begin{theorem}[Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m]
\label{thm:m5-gpy}
$\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_n}{\log p_n}=0$.
\end{theorem}
The quantity $\log p$ is usually called the \emph{average gap} because,
according to the Prime Number Theorem, there is at least one prime on
average in each interval
$(k\log n,(k+1)\log n)$, where $0\le k\le n/\log n$.
With Theorem~\ref{thm:m5-gpy}, one can ask whether
$\liminf_{p\to\infty}(p_{\mathrm{next}}-p)<\infty$, that is, whether
bounded gaps between primes exist.

The Prime Number Theorem tells us that if $p_{n+1}-p_n\not<\log p_n$
infinitely often, then eventually the intervals
$(k\log n,(k+1)\log n)$ would not have primes, so the prime count for
large $n$ would be off. But one can ask what the largest possible gap is,
infinitely often. We have a powerful conjecture, again a consequence
of Cram\'er's model.
% Author check: the source's negated inequality and ensuing implication are retained.
\setcounter{theorem}{20}\begin{conjecture}
$\displaystyle\limsup_{p\to\infty}
\frac{p_{\mathrm{next}}-p}{(\log p)^2}=1$.
\end{conjecture}
Consider the sequence of consecutive numbers $m!+2,m!+3,\ldots,m!+m$.
If $m!\sim x$, then the length of $(m!+2,m!+m)$ is
$\sim\log x/\log\log x$, which is far less than $(\log x)^2$.
In fact, it is dominated even by the average gap.

% Source page 7
Regardless, one must be ambitious. Goldston, Pintz, and Y{\i}ld{\i}r{\i}m
were! They wanted to prove $\boxed{H_1\le16}$.
Their idea is rather benign.
Let $\mathcal H=\{h_1,h_2,\ldots,h_k\}$, where $h_1<h_2<\cdots<h_k$.
It is called an \emph{admissible $k$-tuple} if $\mathcal H$ has minimal
diameter and misses one congruence class modulo every prime.
% Author check: minimal diameter is included in the source's definition of admissibility.
Given an admissible $k$-tuple, it makes sense to ask whether there can be
two primes in $\{n+h_1,n+h_2,\ldots,n+h_k\}$, since $\mathcal H$ misses
one congruence class, so the iterates can be primes.
Suppose one can find a sequence $\{a(n)\}_n$ of nonnegative numbers such
that, for every $j=1,2,\ldots,k$,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)
 >\frac1k\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n).
 \tag{$\star$}\label{eq:m5-star}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
 \sum_{j=1}^k\left(\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)\right)
 =\sum_{x\le n\le2x}\#\{1\le j\le k:n+h_j\in\mathbb P\}\,a(n)
 >\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n).
\]
Hence $\#\{1\le j\le k:n+h_j\in\mathbb P\}\ge2$, giving
$p_{n+1}-p_n\le\diam(\{h_j\}_{j=1}^k)$.
Inspired by Selberg, their choice of $\{a(n)\}_n$ is as follows.
The idea is to choose $a(n)=1$ if the numbers
$\{n+h_j\}_{j=1}^k$ are all prime, because, even without
\eqref{eq:m5-star}, we would at least have an upper bound on the number
of prime $k$-tuples.
% Source page 8
Let $\{\lambda_d\}_d$ be a sequence of real numbers such that
$\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_d=0$ for all $d>R$. Set
\[
 a(n):=\left(\sum_{d\mid(n+h_1)\cdots(n+h_k)}\lambda_d\right)^2.
\]
If $R<x\le n$ and all the $n+h_j$ are prime, then $a(n)=1$.
Expanding the square, one has
\[
 \sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}
 \left(\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\
 d_1\mid(n+h_1)\cdots(n+h_k)\\d_2\mid(n+h_1)\cdots(n+h_k)}}1\right)
 =
 \sum_{d_1,d_2}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}
 \left(\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\{}[d_1,d_2]\mid(n+h_1)\cdots(n+h_k)}}1\right),
\]
since $[d_1,d_2]\mid m$ if and only if $d_1\mid m$ and $d_2\mid m$.
Thus $\sum_{x\le n\le2x,\ n+h_j\in\mathbb P}a(n)$ and
$\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n)$ are quadratic forms with coefficients
$\sum_{x\le n\le2x,\ [d_1,d_2]\mid\prod_{i=1}^k(n+h_i)}1$.
One needs to choose $\{\lambda_d\}_d$ to maximise
\[
 \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)}
 {\displaystyle\sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n)},
 \qquad\text{preferably making it greater than }1/k.
\]
The two quadratic forms need not commute and hence cannot be simultaneously
diagonalised. Also, the coefficients are too complicated for Lagrange
multipliers. Somehow, they narrowed the choice down to
\[
 \lambda_d=\mu(d)P\left(\frac{\log(R/d)}{\log R}\right)
\]
for some polynomial $P$. Perhaps the motivation lies in Selberg's sieve.
% Source page 9
With that choice of $\lambda_d$, they were able to obtain
\[
 \sum_{x\le n\le2x}a(n)\sim
 \frac{x}{(\log R)^k}\mathfrak S(\mathcal H)
 \int_0^1\frac{y^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\bigl(P^{(k)}(1-y)\bigr)^2\,dy.
\]
For the numerator in the ratio, however, they have to evaluate
\[
 \sum_{d_1,d_2\le R}\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}
 \left(\sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\
 [d_1,d_2]\mid\prod_{i=1}^k(n+h_i)\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}1\right).
\]
This amounts to evaluating the inner sum
$\sum_{x\le n\le2x,\ [d_1,d_2]\mid\prod_{i=1}^k(n+h_i),\
n+h_j\in\mathbb P}1$.
We have
$[d_1,d_2]\mid(n+h_1)\cdots(n+h_k)\Longrightarrow
n\equiv-h_i\pmod{[d_1,d_2]}$.
Of the congruence classes modulo $[d_1,d_2]$, let $g([d_1,d_2])$ count
those that are coprime. For each, we have to calculate the number of $n$
such that $n+h_j$ is prime. Here is where we need really strong
equidistribution theorems.
% Author check: divisibility of the product is replaced by a single-factor congruence in the source.
We know
\[
 \#\{p\in\mathbb P:p\le x,\ p\equiv a\pmod q\}
 \sim\frac{x}{\varphi(q)\log x},
\]
but this is for $x\gg q$; here $[d_1,d_2]\sim R^2\sim x^{1/2}$!
% Source page 10
Assuming the strongest such equidistribution result,\footnote{Bombieri--Vinogradov's
theorem (as strong as the Riemann hypothesis).}
they obtained an asymptote for the numerator:
\[
 \sum_{\substack{x\le n\le2x\\
 [d_1,d_2]\mid\prod_{i=1}^k(n+h_i)\\n+h_j\in\mathbb P}}a(n)
 \sim
 \frac{x}{(\log x)(\log R)^{k-1}}\mathfrak S(\mathcal H)
 \int_0^1\frac{y^{k-2}}{(k-2)!}\bigl(P^{(k-1)}(1-y)\bigr)^2\,dy.
\]
But unfortunately this ratio is less than $1/k$. Disappointing!
They had to use the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture, perhaps the strongest
equidistribution statement, to prove the following.
\setcounter{theorem}{21}\begin{theorem}[Assuming the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture]
$H_1\le16$.
\end{theorem}
Unconditionally, they were only able to retrieve an average result,
Theorem~\ref{thm:m5-gpy}.
In estimating $\{p\in\mathbb P:p\le x,\ p\equiv a\pmod q\}$,
the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem allows $q$ to be as large as $\sqrt x$
while still giving equidistribution.
It goes to show how immaculate primes are!
In fact, the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture says that the exponent $1/2$
in the Bombieri--Vinogradov theorem can be improved to $1-\varepsilon$
for every $\varepsilon>0$.
% Author check: the source's summation restriction and simplified equidistribution formulations are retained.
% Source page 11
This failed attempt to get $H_1\le16$ led to decades of fascinating
progress, leading up to the following state-of-the-art theorems.
\setcounter{theorem}{22}\begin{theorem}[Polymath]
$H_1\le246$.
\end{theorem}
\setcounter{theorem}{23}\begin{theorem}[Assuming the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture]
$H_1\le6$.
\end{theorem}
It was argued that one cannot beat $6$ using sieve-theoretic tools because
of something called the \emph{parity obstruction}. Thus this theory is,
at the moment, stagnant. Tao remarks that progress is one or two clever
ideas away!
