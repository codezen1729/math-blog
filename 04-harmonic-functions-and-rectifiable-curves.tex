\section*{Harmonic Functions and Rectifiable Curves}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
Let us apply the epsilon-room method to harmonic functions.

Let $u\colon U\to\mathbb R$ be harmonic, that is,
\[
\Delta u:=\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}=0,
\]
and let $K\subset U$ be compact. Then
\[
\sup_{z\in K}u(z)=\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z).
\]
This is the maximum modulus principle for harmonic functions.

\textit{Proof.}
Since $\partial K\subseteq K$, as $K$ is compact,
\[
\sup_{z\in K}u(z)\geq\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z).
\]
We shall show that strict inequality can never occur. Suppose it does. Then $u$ attains a maximum in $\operatorname{int}(K)$, say at
\[
z_0\in\operatorname{int}(K).
\]
Let $B_\varepsilon(z_0)\subset\operatorname{int}(K)$, where $z_0=x_0+iy_0$. Consider the restriction
\[
x\longmapsto u(x+iy_0),
\qquad x\in[x_0,x_0+\varepsilon/2].
\]
The second derivative test gives
\[
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}(z_0)\leq0.
\]
Similarly, \(\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}(z_0)\leq0.\)
Since $u$ is harmonic, both must be equalities. We intend to contradict this, so let us give $u$ some $\varepsilon$-room. Define
\[
\boxed{u_\varepsilon(x+iy):=u(x+iy)+\varepsilon(x^2+y^2).}
\]


Suppose
\[
\sup_{z\in K}u(z)>\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z).
\]
Then there is an $\varepsilon'>0$ such that
\[
\sup_{z\in K}u(z)>
\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z)+\varepsilon'.
\]
The function $x^2+y^2$ is continuous on $K$. Let
\[
\sup_{x+iy\in K}(x^2+y^2)=M,
\]
and set $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'/M$. Then
\[
\sup_{z\in\partial K}u_\varepsilon(z)
<\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z)+\frac{\varepsilon'}{M}M
<\sup_{z\in K}u(z)
<\sup_{z\in K}u_\varepsilon(z).
\]
Thus $u_\varepsilon$ again has an interior maximum, and
\[
\frac{\partial^2u_\varepsilon}{\partial x^2}\leq0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2u_\varepsilon}{\partial y^2}\leq0.
\]
Adding these inequalities gives \(4\varepsilon\leq0,\)
a contradiction. Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\sup_{z\in K}u(z)=\sup_{z\in\partial K}u(z).}
\]

\textbf{Note.}
If $f\colon U\to\mathbb C$ is holomorphic, then \(\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}f)\)
is harmonic, and
\[
\sup_{z\in K}\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}f)
=\sup_{z\in\partial K}\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}f)
\qquad(\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi)).
\]
Hence
\[
\sup_{z\in K}\sup_\theta\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}f)
=\sup_{z\in\partial K}\sup_\theta\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}f).
\]
By Tool 2, this yields
\[
\sup_{z\in K}|f(z)|=\sup_{z\in\partial K}|f(z)|.
\]
As was stated, a fact about real-valued functions is lifted to a complex-valued function via Tool 2.

\textbf{Exercise.}
The only infima of $|f|$ attained in the interior for a holomorphic function occur at zeros of the function.


The following is a generalized form of the $\varepsilon$-technique.

The image of a rectifiable curve has measure $0$.

\textit{Proof.}
Let $+$ denote the usual concatenation of curves. Then
\[
\gamma\big|_{[0,T]}+\gamma\big|_{[T,T+h]}
=\gamma\big|_{[0,T+h]}.
\]
We claim that
\[
g(t):=\left|\gamma\big|_{[0,t]}\right|
\]
is continuous. Suppose not. Then there is an $\varepsilon>0$ such that
\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[t,t+h]}\right|>\varepsilon
\qquad(\forall h>0).
\]
By the ``linearity'' of arc length, or rather its additivity,
\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[0,T]}\right|
+\left|\gamma\big|_{[T,T+h]}\right|
=\left|\gamma\big|_{[0,T+h]}\right|.
\]
If $g$ is not continuous, its oscillation yields the assertion above. Thus there is a partition $P$ of $[t,t+h]$ such that
\[
S(P,\overline\gamma)>\varepsilon,
\qquad
\overline\gamma=\gamma\big|_{[t,t+h]},
\]
where
\[
P:t=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=t+h,
\qquad
S(P,\overline\gamma)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}
|\gamma(t_{i+1})-\gamma(t_i)|.
\]
Since $\gamma$ is continuous on a compact interval, it is uniformly continuous; consequently, its oscillation on intervals of length $h$ tends to $0$ as $h\to0$. Choose $P$ so that \(|\gamma(t_i)-\gamma(t_0)|<\varepsilon/2.\)
Then
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|\gamma(t_{i+1})-\gamma(t_i)|>\varepsilon/2,
\]
and


\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[t_2,t_3]}\right|>\varepsilon/2.
\]
Again we have an interval on which the arc length exceeds $\varepsilon/2$. Repeating the argument and using additivity, together with the fact that there is a partition $P$ for which
\[
S\left(P,\gamma\big|_{[t_i,t_j]}\right)>\varepsilon/2,
\]
would imply
\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[0,1]}\right|=\infty.
\]
This contradicts rectifiability. Therefore $g(t)$ is continuous.

Fix $\gamma(0)$. By definition, \(\gamma(1)\in\overline{B_{|\gamma|}(\gamma(0))}.\)
By the intermediate value property, there is a $t\in[0,1]$ such that
\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[0,t]}\right|
=\left|\gamma\big|_{[t,1]}\right|
=\frac{|\gamma|}{2}.
\]
Hence
\[
\gamma\big|_{[0,t]}\cup\gamma\big|_{[t,1]}
\subseteq
\overline{B_{|\gamma|/2}(\gamma(0))}
\cup
\overline{B_{|\gamma|/2}(\gamma(1))},
\]
and, denoting Lebesgue measure by $m$,
\[
m\left(
B_{|\gamma|/2}(\gamma(0))
\cup B_{|\gamma|/2}(\gamma(1))
\right)
\leq2\pi\left(\frac{|\gamma|}{2}\right)^2.
\]
Proceed inductively to obtain
\[
\gamma\big|_{[0,1]}
\subseteq
\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^{n-1}+2}
B_{|\gamma|/2^n}(\gamma(i)),
\]
where the points $\{i,j\}_i$ are obtained by the intermediate value property so that
\[
\left|\gamma\big|_{[i,i+1]}\right|
=\left|\gamma\big|_{[i+1,i+2]}\right|
=\frac{|\gamma|}{2^n}.
\]

By monotonicity,
\[
m\left(\gamma\big|_{[0,1]}\right)
\leq(2+2^{n-1})\pi
\left(\frac{|\gamma|}{2^n}\right)^2
\leq\frac{\pi|\gamma|}{2^n}.
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{
0\leq m\left(\gamma\big|_{[0,1]}\right)
\leq\inf_n\frac{\pi|\gamma|}{2^n}=0.}
\]
Therefore images of rectifiable curves have measure $0$.

\textbf{Remark.}
Space-filling curves are not rectifiable. In fact, any curve with Hausdorff dimension greater than $1$ is unrectifiable.


Note that this double induction gives a rather strong result. The picture below shows that Riemann-integrable functions have graphs of measure $0$.

In particular, graphs of continuous or almost-continuous functions also have measure $0$. If we invoke Littlewood's principle, namely Lusin's theorem, then the graph of any measurable function whose support is a set of finite measure has measure $0$, since such functions are ``almost'' continuous. Sadly, none of these capture the notion of rectifiability, since there is no implicit function theorem for continuous functions.



\subsection*{Tool 6 — Fundamental Theorem of Calculus}
Let $f$ be Riemann integrable, and let $F$ satisfy $F'=f$. Then
\[
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).
\]


Let $u\colon O\to\mathbb R$ be harmonic. Then there is a harmonic function $v\colon O\to\mathbb R$, unique up to constants and called the harmonic conjugate, such that $u+iv$ is holomorphic in $O$, where $O$ is some region in $\mathbb C$.

\textit{Proof.}
Assume first that $v$ is the harmonic conjugate of $u$. Then
\begin{align*}
v(x+iy)-v(0)
&=v(x+iy)-v(x)+v(x)-v(0)\\
&=\int_0^x\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(t)\,dt
+\int_0^y\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x+it)\,dt.
\end{align*}


By the Cauchy--Riemann equations,
\[
v(x+iy)=v(0)
-\int_0^x\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(t)\,dt
+\int_0^y\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+it)\,dt.
\]
Denote the two integrals by $H(x,y)$. The function $u$ determines $H(x,y)$, so another conjugate of $u$ can differ only by a constant.

Now let us establish existence. Since $u$ determines $H(x,y)$, define
\[
v(x+iy):=H(x,y)
=-\int_0^x\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(t)\,dt
+\int_0^y\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+it)\,dt.
\]
Can we trace back and recover the Cauchy--Riemann equations? By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(x+iy)
&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac1h
\int_y^{y+h}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+it)\,dt\\
&=\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{u(x+i(y+h))-u(x+iy)}{h}\\
&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(z),
\qquad z=x+iy.
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(z)
=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(z).
\]
Also,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x+iy)
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
\left(\int_0^y\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+it)\,dt\right)\\
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
+\lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\int_0^y
\left(
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+h+it)
-\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x+it)
\right)dt\\
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
+\lim_{h\to0}\int_0^y
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}(x'+it)\,dt,
\end{align*}
where $x'\in(x,x+h)$ by the mean value theorem, since $u\in C^2$.


As $h\to0$, we have $x'\to x$. Therefore
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x+iy)
=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
+\int_0^y\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}(x+it)\,dt.
\]
Since $u$ is harmonic,
\[
\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}
=-\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}.
\]
Hence
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(x+iy)
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
-\int_0^y\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}(x+it)\,dt\\
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
-\left(
\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x+iy)
-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x)
\right)\\
&=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(x+iy).
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(z)
=-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(z).
\]

Therefore $(u,v)$ is Fr\'echet differentiable and satisfies the Cauchy--Riemann equations, so $u+iv$ is holomorphic.

\textbf{Remark.}
This proof goes through even for a disk $D$, hence for any simply connected domain.

\textbf{Remark.}
Let $f\colon\mathbb C\to\mathbb R$ be harmonic. If $f\colon\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ is holomorphic, then $0$ is a harmonic conjugate of $\operatorname{Re}(f)$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, one concludes that $\operatorname{Re}(f)$ is constant, and hence $f$ is constant. Thus holomorphic functions demand more than one dimension to be harmonic, but, as it turns out, they do not merely demand it: they ensure that they get multidimensional freedom; they ensure harmony. In fact, they turn out to be smooth.


If $u$ is harmonic, then \(u_x-iu_y\)
is holomorphic, since
\[
u_{xx}=-u_{yy},
\qquad
u_{xy}=-(-u_{yx}).
\]
Now, if $u\colon U\to\mathbb C$ and $U$ is simply connected, then $g(z):=u_x-iu_y$ has a primitive. Let that primitive be $f(z)$. The Wirtinger derivative satisfies
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
=f'(z)
=g(z)
&=u_x-iu_y\\
&=\operatorname{Re}(f)_x-i\operatorname{Re}(f)_y.
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[
\operatorname{Re}(f)_x=u_x,
\qquad
\operatorname{Re}(f)_y=u_y,
\]
so \(u=\operatorname{Re}(f).\)
Thus $\operatorname{Im}(f)$ is the required conjugate.


\textbf{Remark.}
Suppose $u$ and $v$ are harmonic and defined on all of $\mathbb C$. Let \(u\leq v+M.\)
Then $u-v$ is harmonic and $u-v\leq M$. Let $w$ be its harmonic conjugate. Then
\[
(u-v)+iw
\]
is entire, so \(e^{(u-v)+iw}\)
is entire and bounded. Hence
\[
u=v+k.
\]
If equality in $u\leq v+M$ is attained, then $u=v+M$. ``A function in harmony never dominates another of the same kind.'' The same argument shows that an entire function with bounded real part is constant.
