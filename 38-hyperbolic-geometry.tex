\section*{Hyperbolic Geometry}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
Four questions lead us into hyperbolic geometry and its role in complex dynamics:
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{0}\item What are hyperbolic surfaces?
\setcounter{enumi}{1}\item Why care about them?
\setcounter{enumi}{2}\item What is so special about them?
\setcounter{enumi}{3}\item What does this have to do with complex dynamics?
\end{enumerate}
Let us address these one by one. Riemann's brilliant insight is that geometry on a manifold requires a smoothly varying symmetric positive-definite bilinear form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_p$ on $T_pM$. This inner product leads to geodesics (lines), distances, angles, area, volume, curvature, and so on. In parallelisable spaces, defining length looks natural: glide along a curve and sum infinitesimal distances $|\gamma'(t)|$, using $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\gamma(t)}$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.52\textwidth]{cd7-fig-01.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD7.1}{fig:cd7-01}
\end{figure}
Constructing smooth sections of $T^*M\otimes T^*M$ is also easy, since $TM\cong M\times\mathbb R^n$.
% Source page 2
A Riemannian metric can always be constructed by pushing Euclidean metrics through local charts and patching them with a partition of unity. For a hyperbolic surface, we seek a metric that is also invariant under its conformal automorphisms. First, some context.
\setcounter{theorem}{25}\begin{theorem}[Uniformisation]
Every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally isomorphic to one of
$\widehat{\mathbb C}$ (spherical), $\mathbb C$ (Euclidean), or $\mathbb H\cong\mathbb D$ (hyperbolic).
\end{theorem}
Thus a hyperbolic surface is a Riemann surface whose universal cover is $\mathbb D$. We want to make it a Riemannian manifold in a way that preserves its Riemann-surface automorphisms. Start with $\mathbb D$ or $\mathbb H$, which differ by a Cayley transform.
\setcounter{theorem}{26}\begin{theorem}[Poincar\'e metric on $\mathbb H$]\label{cd7:poincare}
Up to multiplication by a constant, there is a unique Riemannian metric on $\mathbb H$ invariant under every element of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb H)$.
\end{theorem}
% Source page 3
In other words, every $g\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb H)$ is an isometry. This construction is a special case of a general correspondence.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.74\textwidth]{cd7-fig-02.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD7.2}{fig:cd7-02}
\end{figure}
A symmetric Riemannian metric has the form
$g_{11}\,dx\otimes dx+2g_{12}\,dx\otimes dy+g_{22}\,dy\otimes dy$.
For a conformal map $f=u+iv$, the coordinate differentials satisfy
\[
du=u_x\,dx+u_y\,dy,\qquad dv=v_x\,dx+v_y\,dy,
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}du\\dv\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}dx\\dy\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is the differential $df$ in these coordinates. Hence
\begin{align*}
du\otimes du+dv\otimes dv
={}&(u_x^2+v_x^2)\,dx\otimes dx
+2(u_xu_y+v_xv_y)\,dx\otimes dy\\
&+(u_y^2+v_y^2)\,dy\otimes dy.
\end{align*}
Conformality gives $u_xu_y+v_xv_y=0$ and $u_x^2+v_x^2=u_y^2+v_y^2$, motivating the following definition.
% Source page 4
\setcounter{definition}{17}\begin{definition}[Conformal metric]
A metric $ds^2=g_{11}dx^2+2g_{12}dx\,dy+g_{22}dy^2$, where $ds^2$ denotes $ds\otimes ds$, is conformal if $g_{11}=g_{22}$ and $g_{12}=0$.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{cd7:poincare}]
We know $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb H)\cong\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$. Given $w_0\in\mathbb H$, choose $(az+b)/(cz+d)$ such that
\[
cw_0^2+(d-a)w_0-b=0,\qquad
\left.\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)'\right|_{w_0}=i.
\]
For such an automorphism, the derivative at $w_0$ as a real-linear map is $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Thus
\[
df_{w_0}\left(c\frac\partial{\partial x}+d\frac\partial{\partial y}\right)
=-d\frac\partial{\partial x}+c\frac\partial{\partial y}.
\]
Let $g_0=g_{11}du^2+2g_{12}du\,dv+g_{22}dv^2$ be an invariant metric. Then
\begin{align*}
g_{w_0}\left(c\partial_x+d\partial_y,a\partial_x+b\partial_y\right)
&=acg_{11}+bdg_{22}+2bcg_{12}\\
&=\widehat g_{w_0}\left(-d\partial_x+c\partial_y,-b\partial_x+a\partial_y\right),
\end{align*}
where $\widehat g$ is the pushforward. Thus
$\widehat g_{w_0}=g_{22}dv^2-2g_{12}dv\,du+g_{11}du^2$, forcing $g_{12}=0$ and $g_{11}=g_{22}$. An invariant metric is therefore conformal, and unique up to multiplication by a constant.
% Author check: source cross coefficient 2bc and the displayed pushed-forward diagonal terms are retained.
% Source page 5
A positive-definite conformal metric has the form $ds^2=\gamma(x+iy)^2(dx^2+dy^2)$; its associated norm is $ds=\gamma(z)|dz|$. Invariance under $f$ reads $\gamma(f(z))|df(z)|=\gamma(z)|dz|$. Evaluate this on $\partial_x$, which maps to $v=u_x\partial_x+v_x\partial_y$:
\[
\gamma(z)\sqrt{dx^2(\partial_x,\partial_x)+dy^2(\partial_x,\partial_x)}
=\gamma(f(z))\sqrt{dx^2(v,v)+dy^2(v,v)}.
\]
We obtain $\gamma(f(z))=|f'(z)|^{-1}\gamma(z)$. It is a simple computational exercise to verify that $\gamma(x+iy)=1/y$ satisfies this for all $f\in\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$.
\end{proof}
Using the Cayley transform $(z+i)/(z-i)$ transfers this to the invariant metric
$ds=2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$ on $\mathbb D$. Let us use it to construct a metric on $\mathbb D/\Gamma$, with $\Gamma<\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$ discrete.
% Author check: source Cayley transform is the reciprocal of the usual upper-half-plane-to-disc map.

Let $S$ be hyperbolic, with universal cover $P\colon\mathbb D\to S$. For $y\in S$, let $U_y$ be evenly covered. Choose $V_a\subset\mathbb D$ so that $P|_{V_a}\colon V_a\to U_y$ is biholomorphic, and set
\[
\gamma_a(P|_{V_a}(z))=\frac{\rho(z)}{|P'(z)|},
\]
where $\rho(z)|dz|$ is the Poincar\'e metric on $\mathbb D$. Let $\{\psi_y\}_y$ be a partition of unity subordinate to $\{U_y\}_{y\in S}$.
% Source page 6
Patch by setting
\[
g=ds^2=\sum_y\psi_y\bigl(\gamma_a(P|_{V_a}(z))\bigr)^2(dx^2+dy^2).
\]
It remains to check that the pushforward is well-defined. Given $x,a\in P^{-1}(y)$, normality of the covering gives a deck transformation $\phi\colon\mathbb D\to\mathbb D$ with $P=\phi\circ P$ and $\phi(x)=a$. Thus $P'(a)=P'(x)\phi'(a)$. As $\phi$ is a M\"obius transformation in $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb D)$,
\[
|\phi'(a)|=\frac{1-|x|^2}{1-|a|^2}=\frac{\rho(a)}{\rho(x)},\qquad
\frac{\rho(a)}{|P'(a)|}=\frac{\rho(x)}{|P'(x)|}.
\]
Therefore $\gamma_a(P|_{V_a}(z))=\gamma_x(P|_{V_x}(z))$, as required.
% Author check: source reverses the deck-transformation composition and the corresponding derivative/base-point formulas.

This metric defines distances. For a smooth or piecewise smooth path $p\colon[0,1]\to S$,
\[
\operatorname{length}(p)=\int_pds=\int_0^1|p'(t)|\,dt,
\qquad |p'(t)|=\sqrt{ds\otimes ds(p'(t),p'(t))}.
\]

\setcounter{definition}{18}\begin{definition}[Poincar\'e distance]
For $z_0,z_1\in S$, let $\mathcal Z$ be the smooth or piecewise smooth paths joining them. Define $\dist_S(z_0,z_1)=\inf_{p\in\mathcal Z}\operatorname{length}(p)$.
\end{definition}
We now prove an important fact about hyperbolic surfaces with the Poincar\'e distance.
% Source page 7
\setcounter{theorem}{27}\begin{lemma}[Completeness]\label{cd7:complete}
The space $(S,\dist_S)$ is complete. Given any $z_0,z_1\in S$, there is a path $p$ with $\operatorname{length}(p)=\dist_S(z_0,z_1)$; in other words, there is at least one geodesic.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First take $S=\mathbb D$. We may assume $z_0=0$ and $z_1=r\in\mathbb R$, with $0<r<1$, by applying an automorphism of $\mathbb D$. Let $p\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb D$ join $0$ to $r$, and write $p(t)=(x(t),y(t))$. Then
\[
\sqrt{\frac4{(1-|p(t)|^2)^2}\bigl(dx^2(p',p')+dy^2(p',p')\bigr)}
\ge\sqrt{\frac4{(1-x(t)^2)^2}\,dx^2(p',p')}.
\]
Thus
\[
\int_pds=\int_p\frac{2|dz|}{1-|z|^2}
\ge\int_p\frac{2|dx|}{1-x^2}
\ge\int_0^r\frac{2\,dx}{1-x^2}
=\log\frac{1+r}{1-r}.
\]
Equality holds if and only if $y(t)=0$. If $\psi$ takes $z_0,z_1$ to $0,r$, then
\[
\dist_{\mathbb D}(z_0,z_1)
=\dist_{\mathbb D}(\psi(z_0),\psi(z_1))
=\log\frac{1+|\psi(z_1)|}{1-|\psi(z_0)|}.
\]
% Author check: source denominator is 1-|psi(z_0)|, inconsistent with its preceding radial formula.
Hyperbolic balls in $\mathbb D$ are Euclidean balls, generally with different centres. Thus on $\widetilde S=\overline{B_r(0)}$, $r<1$, the hyperbolic and Euclidean metrics give the same topology. Therefore $\mathbb D$ is complete, and geodesics exist and are uniquely determined by two distinct points.
% Source page 8
Now let $S$ be any hyperbolic surface. For small enough $\varepsilon$, the set $\{z\in S:\dist_S(z,z_0)\le\varepsilon\}$ is compact. In fact, if $P\colon\mathbb D\to S$ is the covering map with $P(0)=z_0$, then the restriction of $P$ over a sufficiently small ball is an isometry. Also
\[
\dist_S(P(z_0),P(z_1))\le\dist_{\mathbb D}(z_0,z_1),
\]
since a path in $\mathbb D$ of length $\ell$ projects to a path of the same length. A closed metric ball of radius $r$ is compact, being a closed subset of $\overline{B_{2r}(0)}$. For a Cauchy sequence $\{x_n\}$, there is $N$ such that its tail lies in $\{z:\dist_S(x_N,z)\le\varepsilon\}$. It therefore converges.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
Heine--Borel holds for hyperbolic spaces: $N_r(z_0)=\{z\in S:\dist_S(z,z_0)\le r\}$ is compact for every $r>0$.
\end{remark}
An explicit geodesic construction is sketched below. Let $B$ be a ball of local isometry about $z_0$, and choose a path $p$ to $z_1$ with $\operatorname{length}(p)=\dist(z_0,z_1)+\varepsilon$, which is possible by the definition of the infimum.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{cd7-fig-03.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD7.3}{fig:cd7-03}
\end{figure}
Consider paths $p_j'$ with lengths $\dist(z_0,z_1)+\varepsilon_j'$, where $\varepsilon_j'\le\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon_j'\to0$. If they cross $\partial B$ at $x_j$, choose an accumulation point $x$. Join $z_0$ to $x$ by a geodesic, then repeat.
% Source page 9
We care about hyperbolic surfaces because the other surfaces are well known: $\widehat{\mathbb C}$, $\mathbb C$, $S^1\times S^1$, and $S^1\times\mathbb R$. This follows by checking fixed-point-free automorphisms of $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ and discrete subgroups of $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb C)$; their quotients give all spherical and Euclidean surfaces. They all have abelian fundamental group. Thus a surface with nonabelian fundamental group is immediately hyperbolic: hyperbolic surfaces abound.
\setcounter{theorem}{28}\begin{lemma}\label{cd7:three-punctures}
The surface $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\{p,q,r\}$ is hyperbolic.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
It is a Riemann surface with nonabelian fundamental group:
$\pi_1(\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\{p,q,r\})\cong\pi_1(\mathbb C\setminus\{p,q\})\cong\mathbb Z*\mathbb Z$.
\end{proof}
\setcounter{theorem}{29}\begin{corollary}[Picard's theorem]
If a holomorphic map $f\colon\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ omits two distinct values, it is constant.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If $f$ omits $a,b$, it maps $\mathbb C$ to the hyperbolic surface $\mathbb C\setminus\{a,b\}$. It lifts to $\widetilde f\colon\mathbb C\to\mathbb D$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.40\textwidth]{cd7-fig-04.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD7.4}{fig:cd7-04}
\end{figure}
Liouville's theorem makes $\widetilde f$ constant. Here we regard $\mathbb D\subset\mathbb C$ without giving it its Poincar\'e metric.
\end{proof}
% Source page 10
In fact, hyperbolic surfaces are embedded as submanifolds in every Riemann surface. If $\mathbb C$ or $\widehat{\mathbb C}$ covers $S$, it only remains to consider the cylinder and torus. Removing one point there removes infinitely many points in $\mathbb C$; the resulting cover sits in $\mathbb C\setminus\{p,q\}$, or in $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\{p,q,r\}$.

The following theorem makes hyperbolic surfaces special.
\setcounter{theorem}{30}\begin{theorem}[Pick's theorem]\label{cd7:pick}
Let $f\colon S\to S'$ be holomorphic between hyperbolic surfaces. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{0}\item $f$ is a conformal isomorphism;
\setcounter{enumi}{1}\item $f$ is a covering map;
\setcounter{enumi}{2}\item on every compact subset $K$, $f|_K$ is a contraction mapping.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
% Author check: the source lists these cases without explicitly saying "one of"; the first is contained in the second.
\begin{proof}
For $p\in S$, the differential is $df_p\colon T_pS\to T_{f(p)}S'$. Distances depend on $\|v\|$ and $\|df_p(v)\|$, so it is natural to study the operator norm $\|df_p\|$. Lift $f$ to $F$ as follows, with $F(0)=0$, $\pi_1(0)=p$, and $\pi_2(0)=f(\pi_1(0))=f(p)=p'$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.36\textwidth]{cd7-fig-05.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD7.5}{fig:cd7-05}
\end{figure}
% Source page 11
Schwarz's lemma gives $|F'(0)|\le1$, hence $\|DF_0\|\le1$. Indeed,
\[
\|DF_0\|=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\|DF_0(v)\|
=\sup_{\|a+ib\|=1}\|F'(z)(a+ib)\|=|F'(z)|.
\]
Here $F'(z)$ is a real-linear map $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$, identified with a complex-linear map $\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$. Therefore $\|Df_p\|\le1$. If $F$ is a conformal isomorphism, $\pi_2\circ F$ is a covering map, so $f$ is a covering map and $\|Df_p\|=1$. Thus $f$ is a local isometry; if $\pi_1,\pi_2$ are biholomorphisms, it is a global isometry.

If $f$ is not a covering map, then $\|df_p\|<1$. The ratio $\|df_p(v)\|/\|v\|$ is continuous in $p,v$, being locally smooth in $p$ and bounded linear in $v$. Thus
\[
\sup_{p\in K,\ \|v\|=1}\|df_p(v)\|\le c_K<1.
\]
Enlarge $K$ if necessary, for example to $\overline{B_{2\diam(K)}(z_0)}$. For a path $p$,
\[
\operatorname{length}(p)=\int_0^1\|p'(t)\|\,dt,\qquad
\operatorname{length}(f\circ p)=\int_0^1\|Df_{p(t)}(p'(t))\|\,dt
\le c_K\operatorname{length}(p).
\]
Hence $\dist_{S'}(f(p),f(q))\le c_K\dist_S(p,q)$.
\end{proof}
% Source page 12
Hyperbolic surfaces matter in complex dynamics because they help identify spaces on which a family of functions is stable. More precisely:
\setcounter{theorem}{31}\begin{lemma}
A family $\mathcal F$ of holomorphic functions from $S$ to $T$ is normal.
\end{lemma}
% Author check: the source leaves the hypotheses on S,T and the convention allowing compact divergence implicit.
This normal-family property is central to complex dynamics.
