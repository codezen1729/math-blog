\section*{Parabolic Fixed Points and the Flower Theorem}
% BLOG-CONTENT-BEGIN
Consider a fixed point $f(\widehat p)=\widehat p$ whose multiplier satisfies $\lambda^q=1$. Such points are called parabolic fixed points. It is immediately clear that the expected dynamics should be different. For instance, consider the orbits of $\pm1$ and $\pm i$ under $f(z)=z+z^3$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.55\textwidth]{cd3-fig-01.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.1}{fig:cd3-01}
\end{figure}
The map $f$ repels $1,-1$ and attracts $i,-i$ towards $\widehat p=0$. Notice that it pushes and pulls from two directions. The following theorem tells us that such patterns reappear. For now, no neighbourhood of $\widehat p=0$ can be expected to be a ``basin'', since $\widehat p$ is a sink only in certain directions.
% Source page 2
\setcounter{definition}{10}\begin{definition}
Let $f(z)=z+az^{n+1}+O(z^{n+2})$. A vector $v\in\mathbb C$ is attracting if $nav^n=+1$, and repelling if $nav^n=-1$.
\end{definition}
% Author check: these signs are reversed relative to the subsequent coordinate c=-1/(na) and the orbit asymptotic; retained as written.
\begin{remark}
The equation $nav^n=\pm1$ has $2n$ equally spaced solutions. Also $f$ is locally invertible, with $f^{(n+1)}(0)\ne0$, $f'(0)=1$, and $f^{(k)}(0)=0$ for $1<k\le n$. Thus
\[
(f^{-1})''(0)=-\frac{f''(0)}{(f'(0))^3}=-a\quad(n=1),
\]
and
\[
(f^{-1})'''(0)=\frac{3(f''(0))^2-f'(0)f'''(0)}{(f'(0))^5}
=-f'''(0)=-a\quad(n=2).
\]
Inductively, $-f^{(n+1)}(0)=(f^{-1})^{(n+1)}(0)$. Thus attracting and repelling vectors exchange roles for $f^{-1}$.
\end{remark}
% Author check: the source identifies f^(n+1)(0) with a, omitting the factorial in these examples.
The next theorem explains why these vectors have their names, and shows that all maps with a parabolic point behave somewhat like $z+z^3$.
\setcounter{theorem}{16}\begin{theorem}\label{cd3:asymptotic}
If an orbit $\{z_k\}_k$ of $z_0$ tends to $0$ and $z_k\ne0$ for every $k$, then $z_k\sim v_j/k^{1/n}$ as $k\to\infty$ for some attracting vector $v_j$. The analogous assertion holds for orbits converging to $0$ under $f^{-1}$.
\end{theorem}
% Source page 3
\begin{proof}
We conjugate the dynamics to a translation on a slit plane. First let us state explicitly what we expect. Set $v_j=e^{\pi ij/n}v_0$: $v_j$ is attracting if $2\nmid j$, and repelling if $2\mid j$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.58\textwidth]{cd3-fig-02.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.2}{fig:cd3-02}
\end{figure}
As illustrated, we expect points on these rays to map into one another as indicated by the arrows. The orbit must eventually enter a domain in
\[
\Delta_j=\{re^{i\theta}v_j:r>0,\ |\theta|<\pi/n\}
\]
must contain the orbit eventually; it must also approach $0$ asymptotically in direction $v_j$ ($2\nmid j$). We obtain the necessary bounds by conjugating the iterates to another hyperbolic surface and applying Pick's theorem. Define the biholomorphism
\[
\varphi\colon\Delta_j\longrightarrow\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb R_{(-1)^j},
\qquad\varphi(z)=\frac c{z^n},\quad c=-\frac1{na}.
\]
% Source page 4
On $\Delta_j$, $f(z)=z(1+az^n+o(z^n))$. Shift this to the slit plane $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb R_{(-1)^j}$, and let $\psi_j=\varphi^{-1}$. Then
\[
f\circ\psi_j(w)=\sqrt[n]{\frac c w}\left(1+\frac{ac}{w}+o(1/w)\right),
\qquad w\in\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb R_{(-1)^j},
\]
where the $n$th root makes sense. Consequently,
\begin{align*}
F_j(w)=\varphi\circ f\circ\psi_j(w)
&=w\left(1+\frac{ac}{w}+o(1/w)\right)^{-n}\\
&=w\left(1-\frac{nac}{w}+o(1/w)\right)
=w\left(1+\frac1w+o(1/w)\right)\\
&=w+1+o(1).
\end{align*}
Writing the same calculation with big-$O$ notation yields
\[
F_j(w)=w+1+O\!\left(\frac1{\sqrt[n]{|w|}}\right).
\tag{*}\label{cd3:translation-estimate}
\]
This follows by writing $f(z)=z(1+az^n+O(z^n))$ and following the same steps. This estimate will be used below.
% Author check: source writes O(z^n) here, rather than the stronger remainder needed for the displayed estimate.
% Source page 5
For $|w|>R$, with $R$ large, $|F_j(w)-w-1|<1/2$. Thus $\Re F_j(w)>\Re w+1/2$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.47\textwidth]{cd3-fig-03.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.3}{fig:cd3-03}
\end{figure}
When $|w|$ is large, $|\psi_j(w)|$ is small. Thus for small $|z|$,
$\Re\varphi(f(z))>\Re\varphi(z)+1/2$.
For the orbit $\{z_k\}_k$, this gives $\Re\varphi(z_{k+1})>\Re\varphi(z_k)+1/2$. Eventually $\varphi(z_k)\in\mathbb H_R$ for large $R$, and $F_j(\mathbb H_R)\subseteq\mathbb H_R$. Hence $f(\psi_j(\mathbb H_R))\subseteq\psi_j(\mathbb H_R)$. Call $\psi_j(\mathbb H_R)=P_j(R)$. Thus eventually all $z_k$ belong to $\Delta_j$ for some $j$.

Since $F_j(w)-w=1+o(1)$, putting $w_k=\varphi(z_k)$ gives $w_{k+1}-w_k\to1$, and
\[
\frac{w_k-w_0}{k}=\frac1k\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}(w_{h+1}-w_h)\longrightarrow1.
\]
% Source page 6
Thus $w_k/k\to1$, so $w_k\sim k$. Since $1/w_k=-naz_k^n$, we get $naz_k^n\sim-1/k$. As $nav_j^n=-1$, this gives $z_k^n\sim v_j^n/k$.

Write $z_k=r_ke^{i\alpha_k}$ and $v_j=re^{i\alpha}$. Then $z_k^n=r_k^ne^{ni\alpha_k}$ and $v_j^n/k=(r^n/k)e^{ni\alpha}$. The modulus of their ratio tends to one, so $r_k\sim r/k^{1/n}$. Also $e^{n(\alpha_k-\alpha)i}\to1$, so eventually $\alpha_k-\alpha\in(2\pi/n)\mathbb Z$. But eventually $z_k\in P_j(R)\subset\Delta_j$, and $|\alpha_k-\alpha|<\pi/n$, forcing $\alpha_k-\alpha\to0$. Therefore $z_k\sim v_j/k^{1/n}$.
\end{proof}
% Author check: source replaces convergence to a root of unity by eventual exact membership in (2pi/n)Z.
Having seen this, let us identify the basin of attraction for $\widehat p$.
\setcounter{definition}{11}\begin{definition}
The set $\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$ consists of all $p_0$ whose orbit $p_0\mapsto p_1\mapsto p_2\mapsto\cdots$ under $f$ tends to $\widehat p$ along $v_j$. Since $P_j(R)\subset\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$, there is a unique connected component $\mathcal A_j^0$ with $f(\mathcal A_j^0)\subseteq\mathcal A_j^0$. This is called the immediate basin.
\end{definition}
% Source page 7
The construction above gives forward-invariant domains in $\Delta_j$ around $\widehat p$, but it does not yet show that their union surrounds $\widehat p$; gaps may remain. The domain $P_j(R)\subset\Delta_j$ satisfies $f(P_j(R))\subseteq P_j(R)$, and an orbit tending to $\widehat p$ along $v_j$ eventually enters it. Let us generalise this phenomenon.
\setcounter{definition}{12}\begin{definition}[Attracting and repelling petals]
Let $N$ be a neighbourhood of $\widehat p$ on which $f$ is univalent. A set $P\subset N$ is an attracting petal for $f$ at $\widehat p$, associated with an attracting vector $v_j$, if
\begin{itemize}
\item $f(P)\subseteq P$;
\item an orbit $\{z_k\}_k$ enters $P$ eventually if and only if $z_k\to\widehat p$ along $v_j$.
\end{itemize}
Repelling petals are defined similarly by considering $f^{-1}$ on $f(N)$.
\end{definition}
Clearly $P$ contains exactly one $v_j$; these sets therefore look like petals around the ``base branch'' $v_j$. The next theorem says that the attracting and repelling petals surround $\widehat p$: they fit together into a round flower.
% Source page 8
\setcounter{theorem}{17}\begin{theorem}[Flower theorem]\label{cd3:flower}
There are petals $\{P_j\}_{j=0}^{2n-1}$, each attracting or repelling, such that $\{\widehat p\}\cup P_0\cup\cdots\cup P_{2n-1}$ is an open neighbourhood of $\widehat p$. The sets $P_j$ and $P_j\cap P_{j\pm1}$ are simply connected regions, except when $n=1$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
We cannot simply take $P_j(R)$. Although $\{\widehat p\}\cup\bigcup_{j=0}^{2n-1}P_j(R)$ forms a neighbourhood of $\widehat p$, it does not quite form a flower, so we modify $\mathbb H_R$ slightly. Let
\[
W_R=\{u+iv:u+|v|>2R\},\qquad\mathbb H_{2R}\subset W_R.
\]
Then $P_j=\psi_j(W_R)\subset\Delta_j$ is an attracting petal for odd $j$ and large $R$. Similarly, $P_j=\psi_j(-W_R)$ is a repelling petal.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.74\textwidth]{cd3-fig-04.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.4}{fig:cd3-04}
\end{figure}
% Source page 9
Now note the following correspondence, since $\varphi$ preserves orientation because it is locally conformal around $\widehat p$.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.64\textwidth]{cd3-fig-05.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.5}{fig:cd3-05}
\end{figure}
Thus
\[
\varphi(\Delta_j\cap\Delta_{j+1})=
\begin{cases}\text{upper half-plane},&2\mid j,\\
\text{lower half-plane},&2\nmid j.
\end{cases}
\]
Also $\psi_j(P_j\cap P_{j+1})=V_R^+$ or $V_R^-$, where $V_R^\pm$ are components of $W_R$. Hence $P_j$ and $P_j\cap P_{j\pm1}$ are simply connected. Clearly $\{\widehat p\}\cup\bigcup_{j=0}^{2n-1}P_j$ contains a ball around $\widehat p$, since each $P_j$ contains $|z|<\varepsilon_j$ for sufficiently small $\varepsilon_j$. This is the flower theorem, due to Leau and Fatou.
% Author check: source applies psi_j to petal intersections and calls V_R^+- components of W_R; it also says each petal contains a disc. These statements are retained, not silently repaired.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.60\textwidth]{cd3-fig-06.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.6}{fig:cd3-06}
\end{figure}
\end{proof}

We now ask the question suggested by the case $0<|\lambda|<1$: can we understand the dynamics separately on each of the $2n$ petals? The next theorem addresses this completely.
\setcounter{theorem}{18}\begin{theorem}[Parabolic linearisation]\label{cd3:linearisation}
For any petal $P$, there is a conformal embedding $\alpha\colon P\to\mathbb C$, unique up to translation, making the following diagram commute. In the literature, $\alpha$ is called a \emph{Fatou coordinate}.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.34\textwidth]{cd3-fig-07.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.7}{fig:cd3-07}
\end{figure}
\end{theorem}
Before proving the theorem, let us make a few observations. Recall that $\Re F_j(w)>\Re w+1/2$ and
$|F_j(w+1)-w-1|=O(1/\sqrt[n]{|w|})$.
% Author check: the source writes F_j(w+1) in this recalled estimate, unlike (*).
\begin{observation}[1]
For $\widehat w\in\mathbb H_R$, the maps $\beta_k(w)=F_j^{\circ k}(w)-F_j^{\circ k}(\widehat w)$ converge locally uniformly to a biholomorphic map.
\end{observation}
% Source page 11
By Cauchy's estimate, $|F'(w)-1|<C/S^{1+\varepsilon}$ when $|w|\ge2S\ge2R$. Given $w',w''\in\mathbb H_{2S}$,
\[
\frac1{w''-w'}\int_{\gamma_{w'\to w''}}(F'(w)-1)\,dw
=\frac{F(w'')-F(w')}{w''-w'}-1,
\]
so the modulus of the right-hand side is at most $C/S^{1+\varepsilon}$. Since $\Re F^{\circ k}(w)>k/2$, eventually
\[
\left|\frac{\beta_k(w)}{\beta_{k-1}(w)}-1\right|
\le\frac{C'}{k^{1+\varepsilon}}\qquad(k\ge R).
\tag{1}\label{cd3:ratio-bound}
\]
Consequently
\[
1-\frac{C'}{k^{1+\varepsilon}}
\le\left|\frac{\beta_k(w)}{\beta_{k-1}(w)}\right|
\le1+\frac{C'}{k^{1+\varepsilon}}.
\]
The series $\sum_k C'/k^{1+\varepsilon}$ converges, so the product $\prod_k(1+C'/k^{1+\varepsilon})$ converges, say to $P$. Therefore
\[
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left|\frac{\beta_i(w)}{\beta_{i-1}(w)}\right|\le P,
\qquad |\beta_k(w)|\le|\beta_0(w)|P=|w-\widehat w|P.
\tag{2}\label{cd3:product-bound}
\]
% Source page 12
Multiplying \eqref{cd3:ratio-bound} and \eqref{cd3:product-bound}, with $k-1$ in the latter, gives
\[
|\beta_k(w)-\beta_{k-1}(w)|\le\frac{PC'|w-\widehat w|}{k^{1+\varepsilon}}.
\]
Thus $\beta_0(w)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\beta_k(w)-\beta_{k-1}(w))$ is absolutely convergent, because the corresponding series of bounds does. We obtain $\beta_k(w)\to\beta(w)$ and $\beta_k(w)/\beta_0(w)\to\beta(w)/\beta_0(w)$, so $\beta$ is holomorphic.

To see univalence, choose $k_0$ such that $1-C'/k_0^{1+\varepsilon}>0$. Then $|\beta_k(w)/\beta_{k-1}(w)|>0$, so $F_j^{\circ k}$ is eventually univalent: the positive lower bound is independent of $w,\widehat w$ for every $k\ge k_0$. Hence $\beta$ is univalent.
\begin{observation}[2]
$\beta(F_j(w))=\beta(w)+1$.
\end{observation}
Fix $k$. We have $\beta_k(w)\sim F_j^{\circ k}(w)$ and $|F_j(w+1)-(w+1)|=O(1/\sqrt[n]{|w|})$.
% Source page 13
Thus $F_j^{\circ k}(w)\sim w+k\sim w$, and
\begin{align*}
\lim_{k\to\infty}\beta_k(F_j(w))
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\beta_{k+1}(w)
+\lim_{k\to\infty}\bigl(F_j^{\circ(k+1)}(w)-F_j^{\circ k}(w)\bigr),\\
\beta(F_j(w))&=\beta(w)+o(w+k+1-w+k)=\beta(w)+1.
\end{align*}
% Author check: source's final little-o expression and base-point argument are retained as written.
The map $\beta|_{\mathbb H_{2R}}$ is univalent and satisfies this equation. Also, for sufficiently large $k$, $F^{\circ k}(\mathbb H_R)\subseteq\mathbb H_{2R}$. Extending in this way makes $\beta$ univalent on $\mathbb H_R$.
\begin{observation}[3]
If $\beta(\mathbb H_R)=U$, then $\{U+n\}_{n\in\mathbb Z}$ covers $\mathbb C$:
$\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}(U+n)=\mathbb C$.
\end{observation}
Since $\beta(w)\sim w$, for sufficiently large $S$ we have $|\beta(w)-w|<|w|/3$ for all $w\in\mathbb H_R$ with $|w|>S$. Given $\overline w_0\in\mathbb C/\mathbb Z$, choose a representative $w_0$ with $|w_0|>2S$ and $\overline{B_{|w_0|/2}(w_0)}\subseteq\mathbb H_R$. For every $w$ in this disc, $S<|w|<3|w_0|/2$, and therefore $|\beta(w)-w|<|w_0|/2$.
% Source page 14
On its boundary, $|\beta(w)-w|<|w-w_0|$. Thus $w-w_0$ and $(\beta(w)-w)+(w-w_0)$ have the same number of roots. It follows that $w_0\in\beta(B_{|w_0|/2}(w_0))$, proving $\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}(U+n)=\mathbb C$.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{cd3:linearisation}]
\emph{Existence.} For any petal $P$ and $z\in P$, some iterate enters $P_j(R)$. We have the following commutative diagram.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.65\textwidth]{cd3-fig-08.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.8}{fig:cd3-08}
\end{figure}
For $P=P_j(R)$, define $\alpha(z)=\beta(\varphi(z))$. For any $z\in P$, choose $k_0$ so that $f^{\circ k}(z)\in P_j(R)$ for all $k\ge k_0$, and define $\alpha(z)=\alpha(f^{\circ k}(z))-k$.
% Source page 15
This ensures that the following diagram also commutes.
\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.34\textwidth]{cd3-fig-09.pdf}
\FigureTag{CD3.9}{fig:cd3-09}
\end{figure}
\emph{Uniqueness.} Let $\alpha,\alpha'$ be two Fatou coordinates. Then $g=\alpha'\circ\alpha^{-1}$ commutes with $z\mapsto z+1$, so $g(w+1)=g(w)+1$. If $\alpha\colon P\to U$ and $\alpha'\colon P\to U'$ are biholomorphisms, then $g\colon U\to U'$ is a biholomorphism. Since the translates $U+n$ sweep out $\mathbb C$, extend $g$ to an entire function by integer translations, obtaining $\widehat g\colon\mathbb C\to U'+\mathbb Z$.

Its image misses at most one point. If $U'+\mathbb Z=\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, without loss of generality, then $1\in U'+\mathbb Z$, so some integer $n$ belongs to $U'$. Hence $0\in U'-n\subseteq U'+\mathbb Z$, a contradiction. Thus $\widehat g(\mathbb C)=\mathbb C$ and $\widehat g\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb C)$. By the identity theorem, $\widehat g(w+1)=\widehat g(w)+1$.
% Source page 16
Therefore $\alpha$ is unique up to translation.
\end{proof}
\begin{remark}
Critical points again play a crucial role; we will not elaborate on this. If $\alpha$ is the Fatou coordinate for the petal wrapping $v_j$, it extends to $\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$, and $\alpha^{-1}$ extends to some $\mathbb H_R$ with $R$ optimal. This is analogous to the Koenigs-coordinate situation. For every $\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$ there is a maximal petal $P_{\max}\subseteq\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$ to which $\alpha$ extends biholomorphically, and $f$ has a critical point on $\partial P_{\max}$ that prevents further univalent extension.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
We have $\alpha(z)\to\infty$ as $z\to0$, and $0$ is a parabolic fixed point. The preimages $\{f^{-n}(0)\}$ form a dense subset of the basin boundary, obstructing expansion of $\alpha$.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
In conclusion, the $\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)$ are disjoint basins with $\partial\mathcal A_j(\widehat p)\subseteq J$. Each basin has a Fatou coordinate describing its local behaviour on a maximal petal $P_{\max}$.
\end{remark}
